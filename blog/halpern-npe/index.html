Halpern Iteration Accelerates Solving High-Order Monotone Variational Inequalities

In this blog post, we revisit the classical Halpern iteration in 1967 and show its new applications in designing second- and higher-order methods for solving smooth monotone variational inequalities (MVI). By using a large-step inexact Halpern iteration, we propose a novel pth-order method Halpern-ATM, that achieves a fast convergence rate of Õ(T⁻ᵖ). This improves the best-known results for p ≥ 2 since 2012 and matches the classical extragradient method for p = 1.

1 Introduction

In this blog post, we study the monotone variational inequality (MVI) problem , which targets at finding an solution ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ such that

$$\displaystyle\langle F({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}),{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}, \tag{1} $$

where ${\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a nonempty compact convex set and $F:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ is a continuously differentiable monotone operator. The theory of solving MVI problems has been extensively studied, with numerous papers appearing at ICLR, ICML, and NeurIPS . As a important special case of the MVI, the minimax optimization problem The convex-concave minimax optimization $\min_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\max_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}}\phi({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})$ is equivalent to the MVI problem with the operator given by ${\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})=[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\phi({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}),-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\phi({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})]$. underpins influential machine-learning applications such as generative adversarial networks (GANs), with GAN receiving the NeurIPS 2024 Test of Time Award and DCGAN receiving the ICLR 2026 Test of Time Award . The theory of MVI is thus the foundation of nonconvex-nonconcave problems such as GAN training in practice.

A classical approach to solving an MVI is the proximal point method , which iteratively computes proximal point ${\boldsymbol{x}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{P}}_\eta({\boldsymbol{x}}_t)$, where ${\boldsymbol{P}}_\eta=({\rm Id}+\eta({\boldsymbol{F}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}))^{-1}$ is the resolvent operator and ${\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}$ is the normal-cone operator. The state-of-art first and high-order methods can all be obtained from the PPM framework , including Korpelevich’s first-order extragradient (EG) method , Monteiro-Svaiter’s Newton Proximal Extragradient (NPE) method and its high-order generalizations .

The Halpern iteration ${\boldsymbol{x}}_{t+1}=\beta_t{\boldsymbol{x}}_0+(1-\beta_t){\boldsymbol{P}}_\eta({\boldsymbol{x}}_t)$ with $\beta_t=1/(t+2)$ adds a pull toward the initial point ${\boldsymbol{x}}_0$ per iteration，which gives an alternative to achieving the same convergence rate of PPM for computing fixed points. This iteration is recently studied for first-order methods, resulting the anchored extragradient methods that achieves the optimal $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1})$ convergence rate on the proximal redisual ${\rm res}_\eta({\boldsymbol{x}}_t) = \Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_t-{\boldsymbol{P}}_\eta({\boldsymbol{x}}_t)\Vert / \eta$.

This blog post discovers a new application of Halpern iteration in high-order methods. Our observation is that a smaller proximal residual also makes high-order proximal subproblems cheaper to solve, which leading to new convergence rates for high-order methods. Note that $R_t = \eta {\rm res}_\eta({\boldsymbol{x}}_t) = \Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_t-{\boldsymbol{P}}_\eta({\boldsymbol{x}}_t)\Vert$ is exactly the distance from the starting point to the solution of the $t$th subproblem. Although the complexity of first-order methods is independent of it, $R_t$ is in fact a key quantity in the complexity bounds of high-order methods . For instance, for second-order methods $p=2$, the dependency is $R_t^{2/3}$. Therefore, a straightforward applications of the Halpern iteration in the high-order setting leads to lower inner proximal solver costs, which finally improves the best-known complexity from $\mathcal{O}(T^{-(p+1)/2})$ to $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$.

Within a month following our release, three independent works built upon our ideas to establish an optimal convergence rate of $\mathcal{O}(T^{-(3p+1)/2})$, matching the lower bound given by Chen et al. . The core idea behind these developments is to incorporate an additional gradient step into the Halpern iteration to predict the optimal solution of each proximal subproblem, thereby further reducing the subproblem’s computational complexity. While the derivations in those subsequent works are quite involved, this blog post provides a concise proof for improving the classical high-order lgorithms using the classical Halpern iteration. For instance, in the case of $p=2$, our analysis largely reuses existing tools, making the entire proof of Theorem 4.1 only require just a few lines.

The idea of incorporating Halpern iteration was first discovered by GPT-5.6 Sol (see Appendix for the initial AI response), while the authors simplified the resulting algorithm and wrote a human-readable proof. We hope this blog post deepens the community’s understanding of this fundamental problem.

Notations (click to expand) We use $\Vert \cdot \Vert$ to denote the Euclidean norm for vectors and the spectral norm for matrices and tensors in a unified way. We hide logarithmic factors in the notation $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\,\cdot\,)$. Also, we use the notations ${\mathcal{O}}_{p}(\,\cdot\,)$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{p}(\,\cdot\,)$ to hide the constants that depend on $p$. We denote $D^{q}{\boldsymbol{F}}$ as the $q$th-order derivative of an operator ${\boldsymbol{F}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$. For a convex compact set ${\mathcal{X}}$, we let $\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}({\boldsymbol{x}})$ be its normal cone at point ${\boldsymbol{x}}$, equivalently, the subdifferential of the indicator of the set. We also let ${\rm Proj}_{{\mathcal{X}}}$ be the projection operator of ${\boldsymbol{x}}$ onto the set.

1.2 Related Works

Additional Related Works in Convex Optimization (click to expand) Convex optimization $\min_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathcal{U}}} \phi({\boldsymbol{u}})$ is equivalent to the MVI problem with the operator given by ${\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{u}})=\nabla \phi({\boldsymbol{u}})$, whose complexity is fully understood. For first-order methods, Nesterov in 1983 proposed the accelerated gradient descent (AGD) that improves the convergence rate of gradient descent from $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1})$ to $\mathcal{O}(T^{-2})$, matching the lower bound for first-order methods by Nemirovski and Yudin's textbook . For second-order methods, Nesterov in 2006 and 2008 proposed the cubic regularized Newton (CRN) method as well as its acceleration (A-CRN) and showed that they achieve the convergence rate of $\mathcal{O}(T^{-2})$ and $\mathcal{O}(T^{-3})$, respectively. In 2013, Monteiro and Svaiter proposed the accelerated Newton proximal extragradient (A-NPE) method that improves A-CRN to $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-3.5})$. In 2019, three concurrent works extended A-NPE to high-order $p \ge 2$ and established the convergence rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-(3p+1)/2})$. In 2022, two independent groups introduced novel techniques to achieve the optimal convergence rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-(3p+1)/2})$ for high-order methods of all orders, exactly matching the corresponding lower bounds . Notably, the work uses a bisection procedure to simplify the nontrivial CRN subproblems in $p=2$ , and the resulting second-order algorithm requires only a standard regularized Newton step per iteration to achieve the optimal convergence rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-3.5})$.
Additional Related Works in Monotone Variational Inequalities (click to expand) For general MVI problems as studied in this blog post, the optimal complexity is open besides the simple first-order case where $p=1$. For first-order methods, Korpelevich in 1976 proposed the optimal extragradient (EG) method in 1976 with the convergence rate of $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1})$, which matches the corresponding lower bound . For second-order methods, Monteiro and Svaiter in 2012 proposed the Newton proximal extragradient (NPE) method that achieves the convergence rate of $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1.5})$, which each subproblem solved in $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T)$ Newton steps using a similar bisection strategy as solving the CRN subproblems . Two concurrent works in 2023 showed the same convergence rate is achievable with regularized Newton steps per iteration like the case for convex optimization . For high-order methods, many follow-up works of Monteiro and Svaiter's NPE generalizes it to $p$th-order schemes, achieving the convergence rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-(p+1)/2})$ for solving MVIs.
Additional Related Works in Convex-Concave Optimization (click to expand) The convex-concave minimax optimization $\min_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\max_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}}\phi({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})$ is an important subclass of the MVI problem with the monotone operator given by ${\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})=[\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\phi({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}),-\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\phi({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})]$. Therefore, all the above methods for general MVI can also be applied to solving convex-concave minimax optimization to achieve the convergence rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-(p+1)/2})$ for $p$th-order methods. Very recently, Chen et al. in 2025 proposed a primal-dual Monteiro-Svaiter acceleration framework to achieve a faster rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-(3p+1)/4})$. Maybe surprisingly, this blog post shows that a simple application of Halpern iteration already achieve a even faster rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$, without leveraging the complicated primal-dual momentum tailored to minimax optimization .

2 Preliminaries

2.1 Main Assumptions

We study the MVI Problem (1) under the following standard assumptions .

Assumption 2.1. ${\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a nonempty compact convex set.
Assumption 2.2. We assume there exists ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star},$ such that $0\in({\boldsymbol{F}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}})({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})$.
Assumption 2.3. ${\boldsymbol{F}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is continuous and monotone:
$$ \langle{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{y}}),{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathcal{X}}. $$
Assumption 2.4. Assume $F:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ is $p$th-order $L_{p}$ smooth:
$$ \left\Vert D^{p-1}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})-D^{p-1}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\right\Vert\leq L_{p}\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\right\Vert,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathcal{X}}. \tag{5} $$

2.2 Tensor Steps

A basic operation to leverage the $p$th-order oracle in equation 3 is the following tensor step , which solves a MVI subproblem induced by a local Taylor approximation.

Definition 2.1. Under Assumption 2.3 and Assumption 2.4, for an input point ${\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ the $p$th-order tensor step with regularization parameter $M\geq L_{p}$ outputs ${\boldsymbol{y}}={\mathcal{T}}_{\boldsymbol{F}}^{p}({\boldsymbol{x}};M)$ such that
$$ {\boldsymbol{0}}\in\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{p}({\boldsymbol{y}})+\frac{M}{p!}\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\Vert^{p-1}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})+{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}), \tag{6} $$
where $\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{p}({\boldsymbol{y}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}D^{k}{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})[{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{k}/k!$ is $(p-1)$th-order Taylor expansion for ${\boldsymbol{F}}$ at the center point ${\boldsymbol{x}}$.

2.3 Resolvent Operators and Residuals

To apply the Halpern iteration to MVI problems, we also make use of the following definitions of the resolvent/proximal operator .

Definition 2.2. Under Assumption 2.3, for $\eta>0$, we can define the unique-valued resolvent operator as
$$ {\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}})=({\rm Id}+\eta({\boldsymbol{F}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}))^{-1}({\boldsymbol{x}}). $$

It is well-known that ${\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}$ is non-expansive if ${\boldsymbol{F}}$ is monotone .

Proposition 2.1 ( ). Under Assumption 2.3, the operator ${\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}$ is non-expansive for any $\eta>0$,
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})\Vert\leq\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\Vert,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}. $$

Moreover, it is easy to see that ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}$ solved the MVI problem, i.e., ${\boldsymbol{0}}\in{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})$, if and only if ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}$ is the fixed point of ${\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}$, i.e., ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})$. It motives the definition of the following proximal residual .

Definition 2.3. Under Assumption 2.3, we define the proximal residual for a point ${\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ as
$$ {\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}})\Vert/\eta. $$

In addition, finding a point with a small proximal residual is sufficient for approximating the MVI problem, where the left-hand side below is the strong optimality criterion for MVIs .

Proposition 2.2 ( ). Under Assumption 2.3, then for ${\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}})$,
$$ {\rm dist}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{y}})+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\boldsymbol{y}}))\leq{\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}). \tag{7} $$

3 Main Result

The main theorem below shows a new complexity upper bound that of $T=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-1/p})$ for finding an ${\epsilon}$-solution such that ${\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq{\epsilon}$, which is equivalent to the convergence rate ${\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$ as claimed, which strictly improves the best-known convergence rate of $\mathcal{O}(T^{-(p+1)/2})$ given by the $p$th-order generalization of the NPE method .

Theorem 3.1. Let $D=\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\right\Vert$ be the distance of the initial point ${\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ to the optimal solution ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}$. Under Assumptions 2.1–2.4, for any integer $p\geq 2$, there exists an algorithm (Algorithm 2) that can return an $\epsilon$-solution ${\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ satisfying ${\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq{\epsilon}$ in the $p$th-order oracle complexity of
$$ T={\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Bigg(D\left(\frac{L_{p}}{\epsilon}\right)^{1/p}\Bigg).} $$

4 Halpern-NPE for $p=2$

In this section, we first prove Theorem 3.1 for $p=2$ by introducing a simple second-order method that achieves the fast convergence rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-2})$. Following the ideas in Section 1.1, we apply a large-step inexact Halpern iteration on the resolvent operator, which is solved by a restarted version of the NPE method . We formally introduce our double-loop Halpern-NPE method algorithm in the following.

4.1 Outer Loop: Inexact Halpern Iteration

In equation 4, a simple choice of the anchoring coefficient $\beta_{t}$ for optimal convergence rate is $\beta_{t}=1/(t+2)$ . Then, we obtain the following scheme by applying the Halpern iteration $\beta_{t}=1/(t+2)$ on the approximate resolvent ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{t}\approx{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})$:

$$ {\boldsymbol{x}}_{t+1}=\frac{1}{t+2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+\frac{t+1}{t+2}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t},\qquad{\rm where}\quad{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t}\approx{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}). \tag{8} $$

This inexact Halpern iteration has been analyzed in many prior works . This iteration converges at the rate of ${\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})={\mathcal{O}}((\eta T)^{-1})$ if the approximation error of the resolvent is sufficiently small at every step.

Lemma 4.1 ( Theorem 2.1 of ). Under Assumptions 2.1–2.3, if the resolvent approximation error satisfies
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\Vert\leq\delta_{t}:=\frac{\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\Vert}{98\sqrt{t+2}\log(t+2)},\qquad t=0,\cdots,T-1, \tag{9} $$
then the inexact Halpern iteration in equation 8 guarantees a convergence rate of
$$ {\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})\leq\frac{4\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\Vert}{\eta(T+1)}. \tag{10} $$

The above result has been extensively used in first-order methods, including parameter-free methods , finite-sum problems , and structured non-monotone problems . In the high-order settings considered in this paper, we show that it allows a larger stepsize of $\eta={\mathcal{O}}(T/L_{2})$ than the choice of $\eta={\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T}/L_{2})$ in the proximal point method .

4.2 Inner Loop: Approximating the Resolvent

Recall that the resolvent in Definition 2.2 is ${\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}})=({\rm Id}+\eta({\boldsymbol{F}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}))^{-1}({\boldsymbol{x}})$. Equivalently, the proximal point ${\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}})$ is the unique solution of

$$ {\boldsymbol{0}}\in{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{y}})+\frac{1}{\eta}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})+{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}). \tag{11} $$

For a fixed ${\boldsymbol{x}}$, the above problem is equivalent to solving the MVI problem induced by the regularized operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}({\boldsymbol{y}}):={\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{y}})+({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})/\eta$, which is $\mu$-strongly monotone for $\mu=1/\eta$:

$$ \langle{\boldsymbol{G}}({\boldsymbol{y}})-{\boldsymbol{G}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}),{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\rangle\geq\mu\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\Vert^{2},\qquad\forall{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}. \tag{12} $$

The complexity of obtaining an approximate solution follows from existing results for smooth strongly MVIs . For instance, we can use the restarted NPE/ARE method , which is essentially a second-order extragradient method with large adaptive stepsize $\tau_{k}={\mathcal{O}}(1/(L_{2}\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k+1/2}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\Vert))$ . See Algorithm 1 for the complete procedure, whose guarantee is stated as follows.

Lemma 4.2 ( Theorem 3.2 of ). Let the operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ be $L_{2}$-second-order smooth satisfying equation 5 and $\eta^{-1}$-strongly monotone. Apply Algorithm 1 with the parameters
$$ M=\Theta(L_{2}),\quad K={\mathcal{O}}\left((\eta L_{2}R)^{2/3}\right),\quad S={\mathcal{O}}\left(\log\log(R/\delta)\right) $$
can return a point ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{S}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ such that $\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{S}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert\leq\delta$ in the total $p$th-order oracle complexity of $K\times S$, where ${\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}=({\boldsymbol{G}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}})^{-1}({\boldsymbol{0}})$ is the unique solution to the MVI induced by ${\boldsymbol{G}}$ and $R=\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert$.

We remark that the above complexity of $K\times S$ can be reduced to $K+S$ with a refined analysis, as in the works studying a similar restart scheme . However, the slightly looser bound of $K\times S$ is enough for our subsequent analyses.

4.3 Final Algorithm and Total Complexity

By applying Algorithm 1 to solve the resolvent operator in equation 8, we obtain our final high-order method to solve MVI problems in Algorithm 2. Then, by appropriately selecting the stepsize $\eta=\Theta(T)$ as well as sub-solver parameter sequences $K_{t}$ and $S_{t}$, we can obtain the total complexity of Algorithm 2 as follows.

Theorem 4.1 (Theorem 3.1 for $p=2$). Let $D=\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\right\Vert$ be the distance of the initial point ${\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ to the optimal solution ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}$. Under Assumptions 2.1–2.4, for $p=2$, apply Algorithm 2 with parameters
$$ \displaystyle T=\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(D\left(\frac{L_{2}}{\epsilon}\right)^{1/2}\right),\quad\eta=\Theta\left(\frac{T}{L_{2}D}\right),\quad M=\Theta(L_{2}), $$
$$ \displaystyle K_{t}=\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(\frac{T}{t+1}\right)^{2/3}\right),\qquad S_{t}=\Theta(\log\log(t+2)), $$
then the algorithm can ensure ${\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})\leq{\epsilon}$ in the total second-order oracle complexity of
$$ {\mathcal{O}}(T\log\log T)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(D\left(L_{2}/{\epsilon}\right)^{1/2}). \tag{13} $$
Proof Let us prove by induction that our parameter setting ensures the convergence rate of
$$ {\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}):=\frac{\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})\Vert}{\eta}\leq\frac{4D}{\eta(T+1)}. \tag{14} $$
For $t=0$, we know from the non-expansiveness of ${\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}$ and the fixed-point property ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})$ that
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\Vert\leq\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})\Vert+\Vert{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\Vert\leq 2\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\Vert=2D, \tag{15} $$
which established the induction base of equation equation 14 by dividing $\eta$ on both sides. Now, we assume equation equation 14 holds for all $t\leq T-1$. Then, $R_{t}:=\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\Vert$, the initial distance of Algorithm 1 satisfies that
$$ R_{t}\leq\frac{4D}{t+1},\quad\forall t=0,\cdots,T-1. \tag{16} $$
Therefore, by Lemma 4.2, at each step the resolvent can be solved up to the approximation error $\delta_{t}$ in equation equation 9 under the parameter setting $M=\Theta(L_{2})$,
$$ K_{t}=\Theta\left((\eta L_{2}R_{t})^{2/3}\right)=\Theta\left(\left(\frac{TR_{t}}{D}\right)^{2/3}\right)={\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(\frac{T}{t+1}\right)^{2/3}\right), $$
and, by the definition of $\delta_{t}=\Theta(R_{t}/(\sqrt{t+2}\log(t+2))$ in equation equation 9,
$$ S_{t}=\Theta\left(\log\log(R_{t}/\delta_{t})\right)=\Theta\left(\log\log(t+2)\right). $$
Therefore, the inexact condition in Lemma 4.1 is satisfied for $t\leq T-1$, which implies the convergence rate of equation equation 14 for $t=T$ by the lemma. This completes the induction. Now, substituting our choices of $\eta$ in equation equation 14 yields
$$ {\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})={\mathcal{O}}\left(L_{2}D^{2}/T^{2}\right), $$
which is smaller than $\epsilon$ by our setting of $T$. Finally, the total number of oracle calls can be bounded by
$$ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}K_{t}S_{t}={\mathcal{O}}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\frac{T}{t+1}\right)^{2/3}\log\log T\right)={\mathcal{O}}(T\log\log T), \tag{17} $$
which is equivalent to the bound of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(D\left(L_{2}/{\epsilon}\right)^{1/2})$ in equation equation 13 by substituting the choice of $T$. ∎

This theorem shows that the proposed Halpern-NPE converges at a fast rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-2})$, which improves both the classical rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-1.5})$ by NPE and the rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-1.75})$ via primal-dual A-NPE for minimax problems. In the next section, we generalize this theorem to all $p\geq 2$ and achieve the upper bound of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$ claimed by the main Theorem 3.1.

5 Halpern-ATM for $p\geq 2$

To achieve the rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$ for all $p\geq 2$, a natural idea is to also use the Halpern iteration with a large stepsize $\eta=\Theta(T^{p-1})$ and reuse the analysis in the proof of Theorem 4.1. To ensure that each subproblem is solvable in $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)$ costs like in equation 24, the sub-solver for the resolvent operator should converges at least $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\exp(-\mu^{1/(p-1)}~T))$ for $\mu$-strongly monotone operators, or equivalently (under black-box reductions ), the $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-(p-1)})$ convergence rate for monotone operators.

However, the classical convergence rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-(p+1)/2})$ achieved by high-order NPE does not satisfy the above requirement when $p\geq 4$. In the following, we first introduce an Anchored Tensor Method (ATM) that achieves the required convergence rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$ in Section 5.1 , then we further accelerate it with the Halpern iteration as in $p=2$ to achieve the $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$ convergence rate in Section 5.2 .

5.1 ATM Subroutine

Recall Section 4.2 that approximating the resolvent is ${\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}})=({\rm Id}+\eta({\boldsymbol{F}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}))^{-1}({\boldsymbol{x}})$ is equivalent to solving the MVI problem induced by the $\eta^{-1}$-strongly monotone operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}({\boldsymbol{y}}):={\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{y}})+({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})/\eta$. We introduce a novel Anchored Tensor Method (ATM) that can solve the problem in the $p$th-order oracle complexity of ${\mathcal{O}}(\eta^{1/(p-1)})$. It also implies a complexity of ${\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{-1/(p-1)})$ for finding an ${\epsilon}$-solution in the monotone case by applying the same algorithm on an ${\epsilon}$-regularized operator .

The starting point of the anchored tensor method is the following local convergence guarantee for the tensor step .

Lemma 5.1 ( Theorem 3.5 of ). Let the operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ be $L_{p}$-$pth$-order smooth satisfying equation 5 and $\mu$-strongly monotone satisfying equation 12. There exists a numerical constant $C$ such that The tensor step ${\boldsymbol{y}}^{+}={\mathcal{T}}_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{p}({\boldsymbol{y}};CL_{p})$ satisfies
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}^{+}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert\leq\sqrt{C_{p}\eta L_{p}}\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert^{(p+1)/2},\quad C_{p}:=\frac{2^{p}(5p-2)}{p!}, $$
where ${\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}=({\boldsymbol{G}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}})^{-1}({\boldsymbol{0}})$ is the unique solution to the MVI induced by ${\boldsymbol{G}}$.

Define the local region radius $\rho(\mu)$ and contraction factor $\theta_{p}$ indicated by this lemma as

$$ \rho(\mu):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mu}{C_{p}L_{p}}\right)^{1/(p-1)},\qquad\theta_{p}:=2^{-(p-1)/2}<1. $$

Consequently, Lemma 5.1 implies hat

$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert\leq\rho(\mu)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}^{+}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert\leq\theta_{p}\rho(\mu). \tag{18} $$

It means that whenever ${\boldsymbol{y}}$ enters the local region, the tensor update converges superlinearly to the minimizer. Based on this observation, we formally propose a two-phase Anchored Tensor Method in Algorithm 3:

  1. In the first phase with $K$ iterations, the algorithm iteratively performs the tensor step on the anchored/regularized operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}_{k}({\boldsymbol{y}}):={\boldsymbol{G}}({\boldsymbol{y}})+(\mu_{k}-\mu)({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0})$. The anchoring coefficient $\mu_{k}$ is chosen such that every tensor step lies in the local superlinear convergence region and $\mu_{k}$ decreases over iterations as the algorithm approaches the minimizer.

  2. At the beginning of the second phase, the anchoring coefficient $\mu_{K}$ has decreased to $\mu_{K}=\mu$ and the algorithm has entered the local region for the unregularized operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}$. Therefore, the second phase reaches an $\delta$-solution in additional $S={\mathcal{O}}(\log\log\delta)$ iterations.

To analyze the ATM method, we first give two lemmas for the anchored operator.

Lemma 5.2 ( Lemma A.1 of ). Let the operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}$ with MVI solution ${\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}$ satisfies Assumptions 2.1–2.3. For $\nu>0$, we define ${\boldsymbol{G}}_{\nu}({\boldsymbol{y}})={\boldsymbol{G}}({\boldsymbol{y}})+\nu({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0})$ be the regularized operator, and let ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu}^{*}$ be the unique solution of $0\in{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\nu}({\boldsymbol{y}})+{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}({\boldsymbol{y}})$. Then
$$ {\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}\right\Vert^{2}+\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\right\Vert^{2}\leq\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\right\Vert^{2}} $$
Lemma 5.3 (Stability of anchoring). Under Assumptions 2.1–2.3, for every $\nu_{1}>\nu_{2}>0$,
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}^{*}\Vert\leq\left(1-\frac{\nu_{2}}{\nu_{1}}\right)\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert. $$
Proof The first-order optimality conditions of ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}^{*}$ and ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}^{*}$ indicate that $-\nu_{1}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0})\in{\boldsymbol{G}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}^{*})+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}^{*})$ and $-\nu_{2}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0})\in{\boldsymbol{G}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}^{*})+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}^{*})$. Then, the monotonicity of ${\boldsymbol{G}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}$ gives
$$ \left\langle-\nu_{1}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0})+\nu_{2}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}),{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}\right\rangle\geq 0. $$
Using ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}=({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}})+({\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0})$ and Lemma 5.2, we obtain
$$ (\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}\right\Vert\geq\nu_{1}\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{1}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\nu_{2}}\right\Vert^{2}. $$
∎

With the help of these two lemmas, we carefully design the schedule of $\mu_{k}$ such that the local region $r_{k}$ contracts fast. Let $C_{p}$ and $\theta_{p}$ be the constants implied by Lemma 5.1, we define

$$ \begin{aligned} r_{k} &:= \max\left\lbrace \rho(\mu),\left(\frac{1}{R}+\frac{k(1-\theta_{p})}{2R(p-1)}\right)^{-1}\right\rbrace,\\ \mu_{k} &:= \rho^{-1}(r_{k})=C_{p}L_{p}(2r_{k})^{p-1}. \end{aligned}\tag{19} $$

where $R>0$ satisfies $\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert\leq R$. By this definition, $r_{k}$ is decreasing from $r_{0}$ that satisfies $r_{0}\leq R$ to $\rho(\mu)$. Consequently, $\mu_{j}$ is decreasing from $\mu_{0}$ such that $\mu_{0}\geq\rho^{-1}(R)$ to $\mu$. We summarize the properties of these sequences in the following.

Lemma 5.4. The sequences $\lbrace r_{k}\rbrace _{k=0}^{K-1}$ and $\lbrace \mu_{k}\rbrace _{k=0}^{K-1}$ defined in equation 19 satisfy:
(a) $r_{k+1}/r_{k}\geq(1+\theta_{p})/2$. (b) $1-\mu_{k+1}/\mu_{k}\leq(1-\theta_{p})r_{k}/(2R)$.
Proof Note that we only need to prove the inequalities hold before $r_{k}$ reaches $\rho(\mu)$, or, equivalently, before $\mu_{k}$ reaches $\mu$. Then, the truncated $r_{k}$ and $\mu_{k}$ after that trivially satisfy the same inequalities.
Proof of Part (a). From the definition of $r_{k}$ in equation 19, we have
$$ \frac{1}{r_{k+1}}-\frac{1}{r_{k}}\leq\frac{1-\theta_{p}}{2R(p-1)}. $$
Rearranging, we have
$$ \frac{r_{k+1}}{r_{k}}\geq\left(1+\frac{(1-\theta_{p})r_{k}}{2R(p-1)}\right)^{-1} \tag{22} $$
Finally, using the inequality $(1+x)^{-1}\geq 1-x$ for $x\geq 0$ and the facts $r_{k}\leq R$ and $p\geq 2$ gives
$$ \frac{r_{k+1}}{r_{k}}\geq 1-\frac{(1-\theta_{p})r_{k}}{2R(p-1)}\geq 1-\frac{1-\theta_{p}}{2}=\frac{1+\theta_{p}}{2}. $$
Proof of Part (b). From the definition of $\mu_{k}$ and $r_{k}$ in equation 19, we have
$$ 1-\frac{\mu_{k+1}}{\mu_{k}}=1-\left(\frac{r_{k+1}}{r_{k}}\right)^{p-1} $$
Using equation 22 and then applying the inequality $1-(1+x)^{-(p-1)}\leq(p-1)x$ for $x\geq 0$, we obtain that
$$ 1-\frac{\mu_{k+1}}{\mu_{j}}=1-\left(\frac{r_{k+1}}{r_{k}}\right)^{p-1}\leq 1-\left(1+\frac{(1-\theta_{p})r_{k}}{2R(p-1)}\right)^{p-1}\leq\frac{(1-\theta_{p})r_{k}}{2R} $$
∎

Now, we are ready to give the complexity bound of the ATM (Algorithm 3).

Theorem 5.1. Let the operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ be $L_{p}$-$p$th-order smooth satisfying equation 5 and $\mu$-strongly monotone satisfying equation 12 . Apply Algorithm 3 with the parameters
$$ M=\Theta(L_{p}),\quad K={\mathcal{O}}\left(R(L_{p}/\mu)^{1/(p-1)}\right),\quad S={\mathcal{O}}(\log\log(D/\delta)) $$
can return a point ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{K+S}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ such that $\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{K+S}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert\leq\delta$ in the total $p$th-order oracle complexity of $K+S$, where ${\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}=({\boldsymbol{G}}+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}})^{-1}({\boldsymbol{0}})$ is the unique solution to the MVI induced by ${\boldsymbol{G}}$ and $R=\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\Vert$.
Proof Let us prove by induction that our parameter setting ensures that
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}^{*}\Vert\leq r_{k}=\rho(\mu_{k}), \tag{20} $$
where ${\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}^{*}$ is the unique solution of the MVI induced by the anchored operator ${\boldsymbol{G}}_{k}$ that satisfies $0\in{\boldsymbol{G}}_{k}({\boldsymbol{y}})+{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\boldsymbol{y}})$. For $k=0$, we know from Lemma 5.2 that $\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}^{*}\Vert\leq R$, which established the induction base since $r_{0}=R$ by definition. Now, we assume equation equation 20 holds for the $k$th iteration. Then, by equation equation 18,
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k+1}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}^{*}\Vert\leq\theta_{p}r_{k}. $$
Moreover, by Lemma 5.3 and Lemma 5.4 (part 2), we have
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k+1}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}^{*}\Vert\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu_{k+1}}{\mu_{k}}\right)R\leq\frac{(1-\theta_{p})r_{k}}{2}. $$
Combining the above two inequalities and using the triangle inequality, we obtain
$$ \Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k+1}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k+1}^{*}\Vert\leq\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k+1}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}^{*}\Vert+\Vert{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k+1}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}^{*}\Vert\leq\frac{(1+\theta_{p})r_{k}}{2}\leq r_{k+1}, $$
where the last step uses Lemma 5.4 (part 1). This completes the induction. Consequently, all the tensor step in phase 1 lies in the local superlinear convergence region in Lemma 5.1. We let the total number of iterations of phase 1 be the smallest number such that $\mu_{k}$ decays to $\mu$. By equation equation 19, such a $K$ satisfies
$$ K={\mathcal{O}}\left(R(L_{p}/\mu)^{1/(p-1)}\right). $$
After phase 1 ends, we have $\mu_{K}=\mu$ and thus ${\boldsymbol{G}}_{K}={\boldsymbol{G}}$. Since the initialization of phase 2 already lies in the local region for ${\boldsymbol{G}}$, by equation equation 18, phase 2 can find a $\delta$-solution in $S={\mathcal{O}}(\log\log(D/\delta)$ iterations. ∎

Treating the ${\mathcal{O}}(\log\log(D/\delta))$ factor as a constant, the above theorem shows that ATM achieves a new complexity upper bound of ${\mathcal{O}}((L_{p}/\mu)^{1/(p-1)})$ for $L_{p}$-$p$th-order smooth and $\mu$-strongly monotone operators. This improves the classical ${\mathcal{O}}((L_{p}/\mu)^{2/(p+1)})$ complexity achieved by high-order NPE for any $p\geq 4$. As we have discussed, it also implies a complexity of ${\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{-1/(p-1)})$ for finding an ${\epsilon}$-solution in the monotone case by applying the same algorithm on an ${\epsilon}$-regularized operator .

5.2 Final Algorithm and Total Complexity

The sequence $R_t=4D/(t+1)$ used in Algorithm 4 is defined in equation 16.

The ATM introduced in the previous section achieves the complexity upper bound of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{-1/(p-1)})$ for monotone problems. In this section, we further show an accelerated complexity of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{-1/p})$ by applying ATM to solve the resolvent operator in the Halpern iteration in Section 4.1. We present the resulting method in Algorithm 4. In light of Theorem 4.1, we choose a large stepsize $\eta=\Theta(T^{p-1})$, which indicates that the outer Halpern iteration converges at the rate of ${\mathcal{O}}((\eta T)^{-1})={\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$. We can then complete the proof by providing a global upper bound of the total oracle complexity via a similar analysis to the proof of Theorem 4.1 in $p=2$. This leads to the following main theorem.

Theorem 5.2 (Full version of Theorem 3.1 ). Let $D=\left\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\right\Vert$ is the distance of the initial point ${\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}$ to the optimal solution ${\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}$. Under Assumptions 2.1–2.4, for any integer $p\geq 2$, apply Algorithm 4 with parameters
$$ \displaystyle T=\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(D\left(\frac{L_{p}}{\epsilon}\right)^{1/p}\right),\quad\eta=\Theta\left(\frac{T^{p-1}}{L_{p}D^{p-1}}\right),\quad M=\Theta(L_{p}), $$
$$ \displaystyle K_{t}=\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{T}{t+1}\right),\qquad S_{t}=\Theta(\log\log(t+2)), $$
then the algorithm can ensure ${\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})\leq{\epsilon}$ in the total $p$th-order oracle complexity of
$$ {\mathcal{O}}(T\log T)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(D\left(L_{p}/{\epsilon}\right)^{1/p}). \tag{21} $$
Proof Following the proof of Theorem 4.1, we show by induction that our parameter setting ensures the convergence rate of
$$ {\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}):=\frac{\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})\Vert}{\eta}\leq\frac{4D}{\eta(T+1)}. \tag{23} $$
The induction base of $t=0$ follows from equation equation 15 by dividing $\eta$ on both sides. Now, we assume equation equation 23 holds for all $t\leq T-1$. Then, $R_{t}:=\Vert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\eta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\Vert$, the initial distance of Algorithm 3 satisfies
$$ R_{t}\leq\frac{4D}{t+1},\quad\forall t=0,\cdots,T-1. $$
Therefore, by Theorem 5.1, at each step the resolvent can be solved up to the approximation error $\delta_{t}$ in equation equation 9 under the parameter setting $M=\Theta(L_{p})$,
$$ K_{t}=\Theta\left(R_{t}(\eta L_{p})^{1/(p-1)})\right)=\Theta\left(\frac{TR_{t}}{D}\right)={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{T}{t+1}\right), $$
and, by the definition of $\delta_{t}=\Theta(R_{t}/(\sqrt{t+2}\log(t+2))$ in equation equation 9,
$$ S_{t}=\Theta\left(\log\log(R_{t}/\delta_{t})\right)=\Theta\left(\log\log(t+2)\right). $$
Therefore, the inexact condition in Lemma 4.1 is satisfied for $t\leq T-1$, which implies the convergence rate of equation equation 23 for $t=T$ by the lemma. This completes the induction. Now, substituting our choices of $\eta$ in equation equation 23 yields
$$ {\rm res}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})={\mathcal{O}}\left(L_{p}D^{p}/T^{p}\right), $$
which is smaller than $\epsilon$ by our setting of $T={\mathcal{O}}(D\left(L_{p}/{\epsilon}\right)^{1/p})$. Finally, the total number of oracle calls can be bounded by
$$ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}K_{t}+S_{t}={\mathcal{O}}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\frac{T}{t+1}+\log\log(t+2)\right)\right)={\mathcal{O}}(T\log T), \tag{24} $$
which is equivalent to the bound of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(D\left(L_{p}/{\epsilon}\right)^{1/p})$ in equation equation 21 by substituting the choice of $T$. ∎

This theorem shows that the proposed Halpern-ATM converges at a fast rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-p})$, which improves both the classical rate of ${\mathcal{O}}(T^{-2/(p+1)})$ by high-order NPE and the rate of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{-4/(3p+1)})$ via high-order primal-dual A-NPE for minimax problems.
