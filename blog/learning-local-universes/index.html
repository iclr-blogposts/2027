Learning Local Universes: Neural Cellular Automata as decentralized world models

Can a world model simulate complex environments using decentralized, repeated, and mostly local rules? We investigate Neural Cellular Automata (NCA) as a foundation for world modeling (W-NCA), achieving lightweight and generalizable simulations across diverse environments. To do that, W-NCA uses only 40k–140k parameters, making it runnable directly in the browser.

Introduction

Learning to predict how the world reacts to actions taken within it is a central challenge in building agents capable of planning and interacting within it. In the deep-learning era, early work explored using learned neural simulators in which to perform planning. Later, Ha and Schmidhuber famously demonstrate the use of recurrent neural networks as World Models, approximating, and indeed simulating, computer game dynamics. In this framework, agents can use these learned simulators, or world models, as internal models of the environment in which they learn how to play via Reinforcement Learning (RL). The premise is powerful: machines, like humans, can use internal models of the world to imagine possible futures and guide their actions. Following this objective, a wide variety of world model architectures have been explored, notably recurrent latent dynamics, diffusion-based models, or latent predictive architectures.

Locality offers a natural principle for designing world models with real-world applicability. Molecules in a test tube continually collide and react, gradually transforming the mixture. A pebble breaks the surface of a pond, sending ripples towards the shore, by pushing neighboring layers of water against each other. Birds adjust their movements in response to a quick glance at their immediate neighbors, forming a coordinated flock. Across scales, real-world local interactions give rise to surprisingly complex global phenomena.

Many environments, like the physical systems above, evolve mainly through local interactions. For instance, in the video game Breakout, the ball moves, bounces off walls, and destroys bricks through interactions with nearby elements. However, the game also maintains global state variables that break spatial locality. When the ball destroys a brick, the scoreboard must update in the corner of the screen, regardless of where the brick is located. To do that, the cells around the broken brick must send a 1-bit signal over a long distance to trigger a local change in the digits. This suggests a division of labor: dense local updates to model motion and collisions, complemented with low-bandwidth signals over distant regions.

Can we learn to simulate environments through repeated application of the same local rules,
with only limited global communication?

We investigate Neural Cellular Automata (NCA) as a foundation for world modeling based on mostly local interactions. We introduce World-NCA (W-NCA), a novel variant which extends the original NCA architecture beyond static targets to support action conditioning and sequential generation, essential elements for world modeling. W-NCA represents the environment as a 2D grid where each cell updates its state using its immediate neighbors and a broadcast action vector. The same update rule applies both across space and time, and propagates information in the environment at a bounded speed, one cell at a time. To account for distant coordination, in Breakout, we introduce a small global communication channel, while preserving primarily local computation.

W-NCA demonstrates reliable and lightweight interactive simulations in Breakout, Snake, Text Editor, and 2D Navigation environments. Our world models operate at low parameter counts (40k–140k), over an order of magnitude fewer parameters than other world model architectures such as DIAMOND (~4.4M), DreamerV3 (~8M), and LeWorldModel (~15M). Thanks to this compactness, W-NCA runs directly in the browser, as demonstrated throughout the blog post.

A rich set of emergent behaviors appears in W-NCA models across the tested environments: spiking global signals in Breakout, distributed memory in Snake, structured action embeddings in Text Editor, and conditional inference and generalization to new layouts in 2D Navigation. Strikingly, W-NCA allows for real-time interactions with the environment. Users can edit the cells in the grid to draw new bricks, rewrite the score, or duplicate the paddle, among other edits. Our world model adapts to these changes coherently.

Model Architecture

Macro-StepLocal PerceptionShared MLPResidual Micro-Steps
Stage 1

Macro-Step Transitions

Each cell stores a state vector with three color channels (RGB) and \(C-3\) hidden channels. Together, the cells form the grid state, \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}\). The color channels represent the current environment frame, while the hidden channels retain information across time. To advance from \(x_t\) to \(x_{t+1}\), the grid completes a ‘macro-step’ consisting of several sequential ‘micro-steps’.

Stage 2

Local Perception & Action Conditioning

Each cell gathers information from its \(3 \times 3\) neighborhood through convolutions, producing a perception vector. These convolutions can use either fixed kernels (Sobel-X and Sobel-Y as in the original NCA paper, plus a Laplacian as in Self-Organising Textures) or trainable kernels. The action vector \(a_t \in \mathbb{R}^{8}\) is concatenated to each cell's perception vector, so that each update depends on both the perception vector and the current action.

Stage 3

Shared MLP & Stochastic Fire Rate

At each cell, the update is computed by a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with three layers and a hidden width between 128 and 512. Its parameters are shared across the grid, so every cell follows the same learned rule, similar to a classic cellular automaton. A Bernoulli mask \(M \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W}\) determines which cells update at each micro-step, introducing asynchronicity.

Stage 4

Residual Micro-Steps

The masked update is added to the cell's existing state through a residual connection. Information can reach only immediate neighbors in one local update, so W-NCA performs \(L\) micro-steps for each environment transition. Repeating the update lets information travel farther across the grid before the model produces the next frame, stored in the RGB channels of \(x_{t+1}\).

Training Setup

Environment TrajectoriesBurn-in PhaseGeneration PhaseGradient Step & Buffer Update
Stage 1

Offline Environment Trajectories

W-NCA is trained on offline trajectories collected by running a policy in each environment (see a description of the policies for each game below). A trajectory records a sequence of RGB frames and the actions taken between them. These frames serve as training targets for W-NCA.

Stage 2

Burn-in Phase

To support stable behavior over long rollouts, a sample replay buffer stores hidden states from trajectories generated by earlier model checkpoints. Each training batch samples states from this buffer and perturbs their hidden channels by adding uniform noise and randomly setting a proportion of them to zero, encouraging recovery from corrupted states. Before predicting future frames, a burn-in phase allows the model to initialize its memory and synchronize with the environment’s dynamics by receiving an initial sequence of ground-truth frames.

Stage 3

Autoregressive Generation Phase

After burn-in, the model predicts future frames sequentially, using its previous prediction and the next action at each step. At every macro-step, an \(L_2\) loss compares the predicted RGB channels with the corresponding ground-truth frame.

Stage 4

Gradient Step & Replay Buffer Update

The resulting hidden states are saved back to the replay buffer; a small fraction are reinitialized to maintain diversity and limit error accumulation. Training uses backpropagation through time (BPTT) with the Adam optimizer and a learning rate of \(5 \times 10^{-4}\). Gradient clipping and truncated backpropagation are used to stabilize optimization, and gradient checkpointing to reduce memory use.

Game Environments

Breakout

For Breakout, we train W-NCA on trajectories obtained from a heuristic policy. Under this policy, the paddle moves towards the ball by using the three available actions: Left, Right, and No-op. To ensure sufficient coverage of infrequent events, such as bounces or collisions, these are oversampled to make up 70% of the training dataset. In Breakout, we can examine how W-NCA can combine both local and global communication for optimal world modeling.

Simulating Local Physics

W-NCA learns the local physics in Breakout, such as the ball moving across the grid, reflecting off walls, bouncing off the paddle, and destroying bricks. These interactions involve local changes in the environment that can be resolved by local information propagation. As for the action vector, although every cell in the grid receives it as input, only those cells inside or around the paddle respond to it. W-NCA distinguishes where an action vector should induce a change, while keeping the rest of the static elements unchanged.

Emergent Global Communication

Other interactions, such as score updates, require information to travel over long distances in the grid. However, if information travels only locally, sending a signal from a broken brick to the scoreboard over \(L\) intermediate cells needs at least \(L+1\) micro-steps. A longer inner rollout allows information to travel further away, but also makes training harder. In fact, as \(L\) increases, gradients must pass through a deeper recurrence for every environment update. Thus, to account for long-distance signaling, purely local communication results in more computationally expensive and unstable training.

To solve this problem, we introduce a small global “scratchpad” that allows for low-bandwidth communication between distant cells. After the inner rollout, a max-pool across the whole grid is applied to two of the hidden channels. This simple mechanism provides a short path for information and gradients to flow over long distances. W-NCA learns to propagate two kinds of signals through one of the global channels. The first is a brief, high-amplitude spike, signaling a score update. The second one consists of a half-amplitude and longer spike that signals a lost life.

We mechanistically test the role of global communication by artificially injecting or suppressing signals on the global channel. Score updates and life losses stop occurring when the channel is clipped at its baseline activity value. Conversely, injecting prototypical spikes induces the expected changes without affecting the rest of the cells in the grid.

Real-Time World Interventions

During training, W-NCA only observes trajectories from valid Breakout games, always featuring a single paddle, a single ball, and fixed-position bricks, lives, and digits. To test whether the environment can be edited in real time during simulation, we directly modify the cells’ RGB channels while W-NCA simultaneously updates the grid.

For example, it is possible to draw a new brick anywhere in the environment. Upon collision with the ball, the brick disappears and the score updates, replicating the expected behavior. The scoreboard can also be edited as the game progresses: changing it from '77' to '81' makes the counter continue from the new value. W-NCA even generalizes to digit configurations never encountered during training. For instance, adding a zero on the right makes the counter change from '000' to '001' upon collision. Also, the counter wraps from '99' to '00', a transition never observed during training. New balls and paddles can also be drawn, behaving identically to the original ones.

Sparse Global Communication

Our global communication mechanism, implemented with the max-pool operation, allows every cell to read from and write to the shared scratchpad at once. At every macro-step, all cells have synchronized access to the maximum value across the grid. Such synchronization departs from the original asynchronicity and biological inspiration of NCA models. Motivated by sparse, asynchronous signaling in biological neural networks, we test whether global communication can emerge under asynchronous constraints. To do that, we restrict scratchpad access to a small random subset of cells at each simulation step.

During training, the proportion of cells denied access to the global channel increases gradually, reaching a 90% dropout rate. Our model adapts well to these constraints. Signals propagate locally until they reach a cell with global access, which relays the information through the global communication channel to other cells with access to it. In this way, the resulting model remains playable under an asynchronous global clock.

Snake

For Snake, W-NCA is trained on trajectories from the Jumanji environment, collected using its expert reinforcement learning policy with four actions: Left, Right, Up, and Down. Fruit locations are supplied from the environment at each step, so the model learns the snake's dynamics without having to predict stochastic fruit placement.

Distributed Memory

To simulate Snake, the cells must remember the trajectory taken by the snake to predict how its tail advances. In fact, when the snake is tightly folded, its previous trajectory becomes ambiguous from the RGB channels alone. For accurate simulation, W-NCA must store information about its path within its hidden channels, implementing a distributed memory across the grid.

Snake consists of a small \(12 \times 12\)-pixel square grid, making local communication sufficient to simulate the game’s dynamics. Visualizing the hidden states during the micro-steps reveals an interesting emergent phenomenon. Waves of activity propagate across the grid, carrying information to predict the next game state. For example, when the snake eats a fruit, a signal must travel from the head to the tail, to instruct it not to advance so the snake can grow in size.

Text Editor

In the Text Editor environment, training trajectories are collected with a random keystroke policy including frequent repeated backspaces and line breaks. Text Editor has 29 discrete actions: 26 alphabetical characters, space, backspace, and enter. Each character follows a minimal \(3 \times 5\)-pixel font, emulating a terminal display. Exclusively via local communication, W-NCA learns to generate and delete characters, move the cursor back and forth, and start a new line when enter is pressed.

The hidden channels reveal horizontal bands corresponding to text lines, an internal structure learned by W-NCA to support the editing task that extends beyond what is visible in the environment frame.

Structured Action Embeddings

Action embeddings can be visualized in two dimensions via dimensionality reduction. A t-SNE projection reveals a structured organization by character orthography, in which different groups, such as ascenders (b, h, k), descenders (q, y, g), rounded forms (o, c, e), and narrow stems (i, l, j), cluster together.

Two-Room Navigation

For Two-Room Navigation, trajectories from LeWorldModel are used to train W-NCA. These trajectories consist of noisy action sequences guiding the particle from one room to the other through the doorway. In Two-Room Navigation, actions are two-dimensional vectors specifying the particle's velocity, which we linearly project into the model's eight-dimensional action space. The resulting W-NCA model accurately simulates the environment over long rollouts, capturing both the particle's motion and wall collisions.

Conditional Inference

During simulation, W-NCA does not require all cells in the grid to update. In Two-Room Navigation, we show that inference can be conditioned on red channel activation, allowing only cells with high red values and their surrounding neighborhoods to update. This conditional inference saves computation while leaving simulation performance unchanged. In W-NCA, locality saves computational resources by updating cells only where and when it is needed.

Generalization to Four-Room Navigation

Beyond computational efficiency, we ask whether W-NCA generalizes to novel environment layouts never seen during training. Specifically, we construct a Four-Room Navigation layout consisting of four rooms connected by three doorways, preserving visual properties such as wall thickness, particle size, and colors. Despite being trained only on two-room trajectories, W-NCA generalizes and simulates Four-Room Navigation accurately without further training. The same learned local rule handles the particle's motion and wall collisions.

Bonus: The Physics of Computation

World modeling typically involves predicting future environment states conditioned on applied actions. We ask whether an unconditioned variant of W-NCA can predict sequential dynamics without external actions. A Turing machine provides a useful example: a head reads and modifies symbols on a tape according to a fixed set of rules. We render the tape as a horizontal row of colored symbols on a 2D grid. Given a five-step burn-in, W-NCA must predict the program execution until its eventual halt. Importantly, the Turing machine's internal state is never provided. The model observes only the head's movements and changes to the tape. In this setup, predicting the next frame involves inferring and learning to execute a program based on its traces.

Binary Addition

First, we generate trajectories from a simple binary addition program. Two numbers are written on the tape in binary format, where blue pixels denote 0s, yellow pixels denote 1s, and a single red pixel acts as the separator between them. The program repeatedly decrements the right-hand operand and increments the left-hand one, resolving carries until the sum is complete, halting the head on top of the red separator. We train W-NCA on operands of up to 5 bits. On longer operands of up to 11 bits, it achieves 100% accuracy, decaying to 97% on 12 bits.

Context-Free Grammars

Then we generate program trajectories from the \(a^n b^n\) formal grammar. The tape starts with \(a^n\), a sequence of \(n\) yellow pixels denoting the \(a\) symbol. The program replaces \(a\)'s with a green marker and appends one red \(b\) symbol at the end of the tape. Once all \(a\)'s have been used up, the program replaces all green markers with \(a\)'s, resulting in an \(a^n b^n\) string. W-NCA is trained on lengths up to \(n=8\), generalizing to all tested lengths up to \(n=200\). As \(n\) increases, testing longer sequences becomes computationally expensive because of the long rollouts.

Discussion and Future Work

Our work proposes an alternative approach to world modeling. We introduce World-NCA (W-NCA), a Neural Cellular Automata (NCA) variant in which environment dynamics are modeled as a predominantly local and decentralized process. At low parameter counts, W-NCA exhibits a wide range of emergent behaviors across environments, including local physics, sparse global coordination and live editing in Breakout, structured action embeddings in Text Editor, distributed memory in Snake, and conditional inference and spatial generalization in Two-Room Navigation. Together, these results motivate a design principle for world modeling in which capacity is allocated primarily to local dynamics while low-bandwidth signals coordinate distant regions. This pattern echoes the structural organization of biological neural networks, where dense local circuits coexist with sparse long-range connections.

Hybridizing W-NCA with existing world model architectures such as LeWorldModel or Dreamer is a promising direction for future research. Extending W-NCA with a global latent memory could be crucial for modeling more complex and naturalistic, egocentric environments where viewpoints shift and observations may be partial. Furthermore, W-NCA currently assumes deterministic environment dynamics. This assumption does not hold for environments like Pac-Man, where ghosts pick a random direction at intersections. Combining W-NCA with diffusion backbones could offer a way to integrate stochasticity into the model’s predictions.

Lastly, throughout this work, we train W-NCA on previously collected offline trajectories from environments. A natural next step would be to evaluate whether conventional or NCA-based policy networks can learn within W-NCA simulations and transfer to the original environments. Also, online training could extend this approach by jointly refining the policy and world model through continued interaction with the environment.
