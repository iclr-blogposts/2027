When discussing uncertainty estimation in deep learning, often, the first scenario that comes to mind is of the following shape:

  • the model produces a measure of uncertainty about its prediction

  • if the model is too uncertain, the prediction is deemed unreliable and discarded

This describes the setting of selective prediction : we select predictions for which the model has high confidence and discard or process separately predictions with high uncertainty.In this blogpost we use “confidence” and “uncertainty” simply as opposite notions.

Selective prediction example: if the model is uncertain, an automated medical diagnosis is deferred to a human expert. Inspired by .

Selective prediction covers a range of popular tasks such as misclassification detection and OOD detection that the reader may be aware of.Selective prediction is orthogonal to confidence calibration, which is not the focus of this blogpost. See for a blogpost that clarifies the differences. It is well motivated by safety critical applications where serving a bad prediction comes with high costs.

Confidence scores , $C\in \mathbb R$ are a core element of selective prediction; they convert model outputs into a scalar measure of confidence/uncertainty. A selection decision can then be made, e.g. accept a prediction only if $C$ exceeds a certain confidence threshold. There is much research on these confidence scores , generally focusing on improving their ability to distinguish good outputs from bad ones. The focus of this blogpost, however, lies elsewhere.

In this blogpost, we are interested in confidence scores $C$ that map the softmax output of a classification network into a scalar value,

$$ C:\Delta^{K-1}\to\mathbb{R}, \quad \boldsymbol{\pi}\mapsto C(\boldsymbol{\pi}), \quad \boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta^{K-1}. \tag{1} $$
\(\Delta^{K-1}\) refers to a probability simplex over \(K\) discrete outcomes – or in other terms, a \(K-\)event discrete probability distribution/vector.

which is then used to perform selection based on whether it exceeds a threshold,

$$ g(\boldsymbol \pi;\tau) = \begin{cases} 0\text{ (reject prediction if uncertain)}, &\text{if }C(\boldsymbol \pi) < \tau\\ 1\text{ (accept prediction if confident)}, &\text{if }C(\boldsymbol \pi) \geq \tau~, \end{cases} \tag{2}\label{eq:select} $$

Notably, since the threshold can be freely set, strictly monotonic transforms of $C(\boldsymbol{\pi})$ give equivalent performance.

Softmax-based confidence scores and selective prediction: \(C\) is a function that maps a categorical softmax distribution \(\boldsymbol \pi\) to a scalar measure of confidence. Higher-confidence predictions are selected according to a decision threshold.

The AI research literature tends to take certain properties of softmax-based confidence scores for granted, and so readers may not have thought much about them. The aim of this blogpost is to inform the reader about said fundamental properties in an intuitive, and enjoyable manner.The ideas explored in this blogpost around Schur convexity are established in classical mathematics and information theory . However, for the intended audience of AI researchers and practitioners, they are often overlooked.

In this blogpost we:

  • Discuss fundamental properties of confidence scores (that AI researchers often take for granted)

  • Show how Schur convexity induces these properties.

  • Discuss the prevalence of Schur-convex scores in the literature.

  • Show that Schur convexity nonetheless permits a wide range of selection behaviour.

What Fundamental Properties Should a Confidence Score Have?

If you were to ask a deep learning researcher or practitioner what fundamental properties a softmax confidence score $C(\boldsymbol \pi)$ should have, they would probably say the following two things straight off the bat:Note that in this section we will keep definitions informal to keep things intuitive for all readers.

Property 1: Confidence is maximised for a one-hot distribution, e.g. \([0,1,0,\dots,0]\)

maxC= C ( )

Property 2: Confidence is minimised for a uniform distribution, \([\frac{1}{K},\frac{1}{K},\dots,\frac{1}{K}]\)

minC= C ( )

To many, this will be intuitive and obvious. When a model thinks there is only a single possible outcome, it should be maximally confident. On the other hand, when it assigns equal probability to all outcomes, it should be maximally uncertain.

For the last property, consider a transfer of probability mass from a larger probability to a smaller one, that makes the distribution less unequal, like Robin Hood – stealing from the rich and giving to the poor.

Robin Hood Transfer: Move probability mass \(\delta\) from a larger probability \(\pi_i\) to a smaller one \(\pi_j\), where \(\pi_i > \pi_j\) and \(0 < \delta < \pi_i - \pi_j\). The distribution becomes “more equal”.

Doesn’t it make sense that confidence should decrease after such a transfer? Which gives us:

Property 3: Confidence decreases after a Robin Hood transfer, \(\boldsymbol \pi \rightarrow \boldsymbol{\pi'}\).

C ( ) > C ( )

Although the above property may not immediately come to mind for an AI researcher, intuitively, spreading out probability so it is more equal between outcomes intuitively ought to increase a model’s uncertainty. The literature tends to take the above properties for granted, and so readers may not have thought much about them. Confidence scores are motivated through decision theory , intuition and empirical performance without explicitly discussing these properties. It may seem obvious that a confidence score should behave as the above, but the reader may be unsure of exactly why they do. We will now demonstrate how the last “Robin Hood” property induces the other two, and how it organically appears throughout many of the softmax confidence scores in the deep learning uncertainty literature.

Schur-Convexity Gives Us These Properties

If Property 3 holds for one Robin Hood transfer, then it also holds for a sequence of Robin Hood transfers, with $C$ decreasing at each step.

$$ \text{For Robin Hood transfers } \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)} \rightarrow \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(2)} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}, \quad C(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}) > C(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(2)}) > \cdots > C(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}) \tag{3} $$
Widget that visualises a sequence of Robin Hood transfers where confidence keeps decreasing.

The neat thing, is that given the above, the maximum and minimum end-points drop out intuitively. You cannot Robin hood transfer past a uniform distribution and likewise cannot reverse Robin Hood transfer past a one-hot distribution, whilst all other distributions are reachable in between these two.

Widget bidirectionally visualises extrema.

Thus, Property 3 induces Properties 1 and 2.

Key Takeaway

It is intuitively desirable for a confidence score $C(\boldsymbol{\pi})$ to decrease after a Robin Hood transfer (Property 3), i.e. increasing equality between two classes increases uncertainty. If this is the case, it will also decrease after a sequence of such transfers. This means that $C$ is maximised for one-hot distributions and minimised for uniform distributions (Properties 1 and 2).

Schur-Convexity, Formally

The above behaviour, which we’ve tried to introduce intuitively, can be framed formally as Majorisation and Schur convexity :We note that the mathematical theory is well established, and if the reader wishes to understand it in more detail they should consult the referenced resources. This blog aims to present ideas to a broader audience, so we will refrain from dwelling too long on theory.

Majorisation

For vectors $\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in \mathbb{R}^K$, where $\sum_k x_k=\sum_k y_k$, $\boldsymbol{x}$ majorises $\boldsymbol{y}$ if, after sorting each vector from largest to smallest:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^k x_i^\text{sorted} \geq \sum_{i=1}^k y_i^\text{sorted}\quad \text{for all } k=1,\dots,K, \tag{4} $$

i.e. adding one element at a time, the cumulative sum of sorted (highest to lowest) $\boldsymbol{x}$ stays greater than or equal to that of $\boldsymbol{y}$, all the way. This is denoted:

$$ \boldsymbol{x} \succ \boldsymbol{y} \tag{5} $$

Linking to our above illustrations about probabilities, note that:

  • Discrete probability vectors $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ all sum to 1, $\sum_k \pi_k=\sum_k \pi’_k=1$, so they all satisfy the first criterion for majorisation.

  • If $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)} \rightarrow \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(2)}$ via a single Robin Hood transfer, then the sorted cumulative sum of $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}$ will exceed $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(2)}$ starting at the donor’s original rank in $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}$.Or potentially after in the case of tied probabilities with the donor. As probability mass was transferred to a lower-ranked outcome, the cumulative sum of $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(2)}$ will never be able to exceed that of $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}$, only catch up to it, so $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}\succ\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(2)}$.

  • Consequently, if $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)} \rightarrow,\dots,\rightarrow \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}$ via a sequence of Robin Hood transfers, each distribution will majorise all subsequent distributions, $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(i)}\succ\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}$ for all $k>i$.

  • So if $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}$ can be produced from $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}$ through a sequence of Robin Hood transfers, $\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}\succ\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}$.

  • In fact, $\boldsymbol{x}\succ\boldsymbol{y} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol{x}\xrightarrow{\text{Robin Hood transfers}}\boldsymbol{y}$. Going the other direction, you can show that if $\boldsymbol{x}\succ\boldsymbol{y}$ there always exists a sequence of Robin Hood transfers to get from $\boldsymbol{x}$ to $\boldsymbol{y}$.

Schur Convexity

A function $f:\mathbb{R}^K\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is Schur convex if

$$ f(\boldsymbol{x}) \geq f(\boldsymbol{y}) \text{ when } \boldsymbol{x}\succ\boldsymbol{y} \tag{6} $$

and strictly Schur convex if

$$ f(\boldsymbol{x}) > f(\boldsymbol{y}) \text{ when } \boldsymbol{x}\succ\boldsymbol{y} \tag{7} $$

As a sequence of Robin Hood transfers from $\boldsymbol{x}$ to $\boldsymbol{y}$ implies $\boldsymbol{x}\succ\boldsymbol{y}$ and vice versa, the above (strict) definition is equivalent to our Property 3. In this blogpost, we chose to start with Robin Hood transfers conceptually as they more intuitively connect with confidence score behaviour compared to the formal definitions of majorisation and Schur convexity. In the remainder of the blog we will refer to Property 3 as Schur convexity; however, we want to emphasise the underlying intuition – Robin Hood transfers that increase inequality also decrease confidence/increase uncertainty. In fact, the standard criterion for Schur convexity tests precisely this (infinitesimally):

Schur–Ostrowski Criterion

$f$ is Schur convex if,

$$ (x_i-x_j) \left( \frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_i} - \frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_j} \right) \geq 0 \quad \text{for all } i,j \text{, and all } \boldsymbol{x}\in \mathbb{R}^K \text{ where } \sum_{k} x_k=\text{constant}, \tag{8} $$

i.e. an infinitesimal transfer of mass from $j\rightarrow i$ induces a change in $f$ that is positive if $x_i>x_j$ and negative if $x_i<x_j$ (or zero).

Finally, we note the difference between strict and non-strict Schur convexity. We have illustrated our intuitions with the strict version, i.e. a (sequence of) Robin Hood transfer(s) always reduces confidence, whilst non-strict Schur convexity permits confidence to stay constant. An example of a non-strict Schur convex score would be the maximum of the softmax $C=\max_k \pi_k$, where transfers between non-max outcomes do not change the score. On consequence of this is the uniqueness of Properties 1 and 2 :

Property strictly Schur-convex Schur-convex
3: (sequence of) Robin Hood transfer(s) decreases score Cannot increase score
1: one-hot distribution(s) unique global maximiser(s) global maximiser(s), unique or non-unique
2: uniform distribution unique global minimiser global minimiser, unique or non-unique

What’s interesting is that not a single paper explicitly discusses Schur convexity. It seems like Schur-convexity has been organically selected for by researchers, without them necessarily understanding that it fundamentally determines some important properties of the scores that they propose.

Many confidence scores inherit Schur convexity via the following result: any (monotone transform of a) permutation-invariant convex function is Schur-convex . A straightforward example is a confidence score of the form,

$$ C(\boldsymbol{\pi})=g\left(\sum_k f(\pi_k) \right) \tag{9}\label{eq:convex-sum} $$

where $f$ is convex and $g$ is non-decreasing monotone – you can see that all the listed scores in the above table follow this form.

The established theoretical and practical frameworks that researchers derive confidence scores from often contain the properties of permutation invariance and convexity, naturally giving rise to Schur convexity. Classes/outcomes are often treated symmetrically as a practical default giving scores permutation invariance – this means scores don’t require class-specific knowledge, improving portability across tasks and ease of deployment. Convexity arises in Bayesian decision theory and Proper Scoring Rules Both MSP and the negative generalised entropies associated with proper scoring rules arise from maximising an expected utility under distribution \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\). Since the expected utility of a given decision is a linear function of the outcome probabilities, its maximum over all possible decisions is convex in \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\). through which many of the above scores can be characterised.We won’t dwell on proper scoring rules here, however, it may interest the reader that many confidence scores in the literature can be cast as negative generalised entropies within this theoretical framework . Most familiar entropies from information theory, where researchers often lift scores from, are of the form in Equation \ref{eq:convex-sum} .

Researchers also like to explicitly construct scores as divergences from the uniform distribution (e.g. Gradnorm , GEN and Self-Certainty ), seeking to minimise confidence at the uniform and maximise it at one-hot distributions (Properties 1 and 2). Interestingly, the three listed examples all end up satisfying the notion of Schur convexity as well.

We Still Need Empirical Comparison

Although Schur convexity gives a confidence score a number of desirable properties, it only constrains ranking behaviour between distributions that majorise each other – for other confidence comparisons it is completely permissive. This is illustrated in the following widget, where we use a score parameterised by power $\alpha$ to demonstrate how ranking behaviour can vary whilst Schur convexity is maintained.Incidentally, we remark that this power score is able to encompass the ranking behaviour of a large range of existing confidence scores, as indicated on the widget. Cycling through the modes, note that as $\alpha$ varies:

  1. The confidence quantile rankings of different $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ vary wildly.

  2. The relative ranking of $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ that majorise each other stays fixed due to Schur convexity.

  3. The relative ranking of $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ that do not majorise each other can change.

Widget that visualises the contours of a score over the simplex. Shows how behaviour can vary considerably even with Schur convexity.

Thus, for developing effective confidence scores for selective prediction, Schur convexity is not sufficient. We still need experimental evaluation to determine the best ranking behaviour for a given task with a given distribution over softmax outputs $\boldsymbol{\pi}$.

Key Takeaway

Although Schur convexity gives a confidence score desirable properties, it is not sufficient to determine ranking performance. For that, we still need empirical evaluations on task-specific distributions over softmax outputs.

Additional Thoughts

Sequences of Probabilities

In the age of large language models, uncertainty scores over sequences of softmax outputs are becoming increasingly pertinent. It turns out we can also define a sequence-wise notion of Schur convexity, based on row-wise majorisation :

Sequence Schur Convexity

For two sequences of probability vectors of length $T$, $\boldsymbol{\Pi}=(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(T)})$ and $\boldsymbol{Q}=(\boldsymbol{q}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}^{(T)})$, a sequence confidence score $C_{\mathrm{seq}}$ is sequence Schur convex if

$$ C_{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{P}) \geq C_{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{Q}) \text{ when } \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(t)} \succ \boldsymbol{q}^{(t)} \text{ for all } t=1,\dots,T, \tag{10} $$

i.e. $\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ majorises $\boldsymbol{Q}$ comparing vectors at each sequence position. Intuitively, a (sequence of) Robin Hood transfer(s) at any position in the sequence should reduce confidence.

One can straightforwardly construct a sequence Schur convex score by aggregating a position-wise score over sequence,

$$ C_{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{P}) = A\left(C(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)}),\ldots, C(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(T)})\right), \tag{11} $$

as long as the aggregation function $A$ is monotonically non-decreasing in each of its arguments, e.g. sum, mean, max, product (if $C\geq 0$). Indeed many existing sequence confidence scores achieve sequence Schur convexity by doing this such as negative mean token entropy , mean Rényi entropy , and self-certainty (without mentioning Schur convexity).

Not All Confidence Scores Are Schur Convex

Confidence score designers are free to forgo Schur convexity as a property, i.e. Robin Hood transfers mustn’t increase confidence, and there are some examples of this in the literature. Generative perplexity evaluates on the generated tokens, so a Robin Hood transfer into a generated token’s probability can increase confidence even if the softmax becomes more equal. The margin between the top 2 probabilities in $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ , keeps the maximum and minimum at one hot and uniform, however, a Robin Hood transfer out of the 2nd largest probability will increase confidence. Scores that treat specific classes/outcomes differently are inherently not permutation invariant, and so are not Schur convex. Ultimately, ranking performance is the downstream goal – Schur convexity provides desirable properties, but they are not essential or necessarily optimal.
