Aligning VDMs and MDMs through Maths and Code

Masked and Variational Diffusion Models (MDMs and VDMs) are typically applied to either text or image generation tasks respectively, and so can look quite different at a first glance. We organise both model families into common components, in both their maths and code, so that it becomes clear where their similarities and differences lie, and demonstrate this across image generation, text generation and Sudoku solving, where swapping one component at a time reveals their effect on the model. In doing so, we hope readers leave with a stronger mental model of diffusion and focus on the modelling choices that matter, instead of getting caught up in implementation details that do not.

Introduction

Masked diffusion models (MDMs) have recently gained popularity for their ability to generate text in parallel instead of one token at a time . MDMs learn to generate data by reversing a corruption process over discrete values, which progressively replaces clean data with mask tokens. In contrast, Gaussian diffusion models, such as variational diffusion models (VDMs) , corrupt and generate continuous values with Gaussian perturbations. These models are commonly used for image generation. Like many reading this, we first learned about diffusion through image generation via DDPMs and VDMs, so learning about their discrete counterparts initially felt alienWe write about VDMs instead of DDPMs because we believe their formulation can be derived and reasoned about more easily and many recent advancements have built on top of it, e.g. high-fidelity and high-resolution image synthesis and diffusion LLMs, whose masked objectives follow VDM's continuous time formulation.. As such, we write this post to elucidate the similarities and difference between these two diffusion model families.

When we get stuck on a paper, some of us may turn to the code for clarity. Matching an equation to a few lines of Python, or spotting a connection to an implementation we already know, often helps things click. Yet discrete diffusion codebases can introduce another layer of unfamiliarity: different method names, different implementation choices, and seemingly fewer conceptual connections to the DDPM , DiT and SiT codebases we know and love. Others may prefer to get their heads around the maths first. However, connecting this back to software isn’t always straightforward, as naming conventions may not correspond to equations and implementations may rely on implicit mathematical identities.

In this post, we’ll work through VDMs and MDMs side by side, connecting their equations to a shared implementation. We present the maths in a way that more directly aligns with their implementations and reveals similarities between these frameworks. Additionally, we outline a software framework which separates these models into three components: a noise schedule, a denoiser and the core diffusion formulation. This means we can disentangle what each component controls, how they interact, and what can be changed independently. Our framework enables shared code for training, sampling and likelihood-bound estimation across both diffusion families, such that only the differences that really matter between VDM and MDMs need to be implemented. Through case studies on image generation, text generation and Sudoku solving, we investigate theoretical properties and show which components can be reused across diffusion families and tasks. We hope this helps to build a mental model that aligns the maths with the code, which you can carry into the next paper you read.

Our code is available here, and model weights will be released upon acceptance.

Preliminaries

Diffusion models share the same components: a noise schedule, a denoiser and the core diffusion formulation. The schedule specifies the corruption level, the denoiser predicts clean data, and the model formulation defines the corruption distributions, generative transitions, the objective and a bound on the data likelihood. We introduce these components at a high level here and provide concrete definitions for VDMs and MDMs in the next section.

Let \(\mathbf{x}\) denote clean data and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) a corrupted state at time \(t\in[0,1]\). Corruption, via the fixed forward process, proceeds from \(t = 0\) to \(t=1\), following the Markov chain

\[\mathbf{x} \rightarrow \mathbf{z}_0 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow \mathbf{z}_1\]

while generation, via the learned reverse process, occurs in the opposite direction,

\[\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{z}_0 \leftarrow \cdots \leftarrow \mathbf{z}_1\]

We use \(q(\cdot)\) for forward distributions and \(p_\theta(\cdot)\) for the learned reverse distributions.

Noise Schedule

The noise schedule \(\mathcal{S}(\cdot)\) maps time \(t\) to a set of parameters describing the corruption level,

\[\mathcal{S}(t) = \{\nu_t, \ldots\},\]

which parameterise the forward distributions. All schedules return \(\nu_t\), derived from the other schedule parameters, which describes the corruption level at time \(t\). Here its endpoints at \(t = 0\) and \(t = 1\) are denoted \(\nu_{\text{min}}\) and \(\nu_{\text{max}}\), respectively. The time \(t\) should be interpreted as a coordinate along the diffusion path, while the noise schedule determines the corruption level at \(t\). Hence, two schedules may map the same \(t\) to different corruption levels, \(\mathcal{S}_1(t) \neq \mathcal{S}_2(t)\).

Denoiser

A denoising neural network estimates clean data from a corrupted state at time \(t\),

\[\hat{\textbf{x}} = \hat{\textbf{x}}_\theta(\textbf{z}_t, \nu_t),\]

where \(\nu_t\) is provided by the noise schedule. In many works, the denoiser is conditioned directly on time \(t\) as opposed to \(\nu_t\) . However, \(t\) may index noise levels differently for each noise schedule. By conditioning on \(\nu_t\), our denoiser is given the same conditioning values for the same corruption level, regardless of the noise schedule. This design choice will help us understand properties of likelihood-bound estimation.

Diffusion Formulation

The diffusion formulation specifies how data are corrupted and how the denoiser’s predictions define reverse transitions. Together with the noise schedule and denoiser, these definitions give us common procedures for training, sampling and likelihood-bound estimation.

Corruption in the Forward Direction

The forward marginal defines the distribution over a corrupted state \(\mathbf{z}_t\) given clean data \(\mathbf{x}\),

\[q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x}).\]

At \(t = 1\), where we have the most corruption, the marginal is no longer dependent on \(\mathbf{x}\), \(q(\mathbf{z}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) \approx q(\mathbf{z}_1)\). The forward marginal lets us sample \(\mathbf{z}_t\) directly from \(\mathbf{x}\). To sample between any two timesteps \(s < t\), we define two additional forward distributions:

  • the forward transition \(q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{z}_s)\) lets us sample a more corrupted \(\mathbf{z}_t\) from a given \(\mathbf{z}_s\),
  • and the forward posterior \(q(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x})\) lets us sample a less corrupted \(\mathbf{z}_s\) given a more corrupted \(\mathbf{z}_t\) and corresponding clean data \(\mathbf{x}\).

Generation in the Reverse Direction

We generate new data by ancestral samplingSampling in DDPM is a type of ancestral sampling. However, as VDMs are trained in continuous time, our steps need not be defined via the same discrete steps as DDPM.. This begins by sampling a simple terminal distribution \(p(\mathbf{z}_1) = q(\mathbf{z}_1)\) and then sampling the learned reverse transitions \(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t)\) over a sequence of decreasing diffusion times \(1 = t_N > t_{N-1} > \cdots > t_1 > t_0 = 0\),

\[\mathbf{z}_1 \sim p(\mathbf{z}_1) = q(\mathbf{z}_1), \qquad \mathbf{z}_s \sim p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t), \qquad 0 \leq s < t \leq 1.\]

The reverse transitions are defined in terms of the forward posterior. If the clean data \(\mathbf{x}\) were known, the forward posterior would define how to move from \(\mathbf{z}_t\) to a less noisy state \(\mathbf{z}_s\). For generation, we instead estimate \(\mathbf{x}\) from the current state \(\mathbf{z}_t\) using a neural network \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\cdot)\),

\[p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t) = q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)).\]

Training

We train the generative model by minimising a negative evidence lower bound (NELBO),

\[-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \leq \text{NELBO}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diffusion}},\]

with each term defined as follows,

\[\begin{gathered} \mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}} = D_\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(\mathbf{z}_1 \mid \mathbf{x})\,\Vert\,p(\mathbf{z}_1)\right), \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} = \mathrm{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_0 \mid \mathbf{x})}\left[-\log p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z}_0)\right], \\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}} = \sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathrm{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)} \mid \mathbf{x})} \left[D_\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)} \mid \mathbf{z}_{t(i)}, \mathbf{x}) \,\Vert\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)} \mid \mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\right)\right], \end{gathered}\]

where \(t(i) = i / T\) and \(s(i) = (i - 1)/T\) denote two adjacent timesteps, with \(s(i) < t(i)\) and step index \(i \in \{1, \cdots, T\}\). We omit \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}\) as they are small in practice. In the limit \(T \rightarrow \infty\), the remaining \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}\) term has a common form in both VDMs and MDMs,

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diffusion}}(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z}_t\sim q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}} \left[\omega(t)\,\mathcal{E}\!\left(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)\right)\right] \end{aligned}\]

where \(\mathcal{E}(\cdot)\) measures prediction error and \(\omega(t)\) weights its contribution. Training minimises this objective using a Monte Carlo estimate.

Likelihood-Bound Estimation

A Monte Carlo estimate of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}\) gives the denoiser’s contribution to the \(\mathrm{NELBO}(\mathbf{x})\), which we call the denoising bound. Both the forward marginal \(q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x})\) and the denoiser depend on \(t\) only through the corruption level \(\nu_t\), and the loss weight in both MDMs and VDMs can be factorised as \(\omega(t) = \nu_t' \; \tilde{\omega}(\nu_t)\)For VDMs, $\nu_t = \log \mathrm{SNR}(t)$, so $\nu_t' = \mathrm{SNR}'(t) / \mathrm{SNR}(t)$ and $\tilde{\omega}(\nu) = -\tfrac{1}{2}e^{\nu}$.
For MDMs, $\nu_t = -\log \alpha_t$, so $\nu_t' = -\alpha_t' / \alpha_t$ and $\tilde{\omega}(\nu) = \alpha / (1 - \alpha) = 1 / (e^{\nu} - 1)$.. Changing variables from \(t\) to \(\nu\) therefore gives

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}(\mathbf{x}) &= \int_0^1 \omega(t) \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x})} \left[ \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t)) \right] dt \\ &= \int_0^1 \nu_t' \; \tilde{\omega}(\nu_t) \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x})} \left[ \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t)) \right] dt \\ &= \int_{\nu_{\min}}^{\nu_{\max}} \tilde{\omega}(\nu) \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{\nu} \mid \mathbf{x})} \left[ \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{\nu}, \nu)) \right] d\nu, \end{aligned}\]

where the noise schedule only appears at endpoints \(\nu_{\min}\) and \(\nu_{\max}\). Therefore, evaluating a trained denoiser under any noise schedule with the same endpoints, even one it was not trained on, gives the same expected denoising bound, i.e. the denoising bound is invariant to the noise schedule For a deeper understanding of this, we refer readers to Section 5.1 and Fig 4 in , Section 3.2 in and Appendix E.11 in .. However, the variance of its Monte Carlo estimate depends on the shape of the schedule and will vary .

Aligning VDMs and MDMs

We now make each component from the preliminaries concrete for VDMs and MDMs, writing their maths side by side to make their similarities and differences clear.

  • \(\alpha_t\) (and \(\sigma_t\) for VDMs) set the corruption level in the forward distributions,
  • \(\nu_t\) is a function of corruption level (\(\alpha_t, \sigma_t\)) on which the denoiser is conditioned.

This leaves slightly different values of $\nu_{\max}$ across schedules ($\approx 6.5$ to $13.6$ for $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$). However, the empirical difference in the denoising bound observed in our experiments is negligible..

Denoiser

The denoiser composes an input adapter \(A_{\text{in}}(\cdot)\), a neural network \(\mathrm{NN}_\theta(\cdot)\), and an output adapter \(A_{\text{out}}(\cdot)\). The input adapter transforms the corrupted state \(\mathbf{z}_t\) into a representation which is usable by the network, \(\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{in}}\). The output adapter transforms the network’s outputs \(\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{feat}}\) to a format usable by the diffusion formulation \(\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{out}}\):

\[\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{in}} = A_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{z}_t), \qquad \mathbf{h}_t^{\text{feat}} = \text{NN}_\theta(\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{in}}, \nu_t), \qquad \mathbf{h}_t^{\text{out}} = A_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{feat}}, \mathbf{z}_t).\]

Here, the output adapter also gets \(\mathbf{z}_t\) as a reference, if needed, to construct \(\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{out}}\).

The output \(\mathbf{h}_t^{\mathrm{out}}\) is turned into a clean data prediction \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) which the diffusion formulation uses:

  • For VDMs, the denoiser outputs the velocity \(\mathbf{h}_t^{\mathrm{out}} = \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) , which we convert to \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) using the noise schedule parameters, \(\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \alpha_t \mathbf{z}_t - \sigma_t \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\)To keep the parallel with MDMs, we use an $\mathbf{x}$-loss for VDMs, rather than an $\pmb{\epsilon}$ or $\mathbf{v}$-loss. Since $\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} = -\sigma_t(\mathbf{v}_t - \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t)$ and $e^{\nu_t}\sigma_t^2 = \alpha_t^2$, the weighted error can be rewritten in terms of the denoiser's output: $-\tfrac{1}{2} e^{\nu_t} \nu_t' \lVert \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert^2_2 = -\tfrac{1}{2} \alpha_t^2 \nu_t'\lVert \mathbf{v}_t - \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t \rVert^2_2$. The weight on the right is bounded since $\alpha_t \leq 1$, whereas a denoiser outputting $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ directly would carry the weight on the left, which grows exponentially in $\nu_t$ and destabilises gradients at low noise..
  • For MDMs, the denoiser outputs \(K\) logits at each position \(d\), \(\hat{x}_d = \mathbf{h}_{t,d}^{\mathrm{out}}\). The [MASK] logit is set to \(-\infty\) so that masks are never predicted.

Diffusion Formulation

In the preliminaries, we generically outlined the roles of the forward distributions, reverse transitions and training objective. The table below fills these in for VDMs and MDMs.

We deviate from the notation of the original papers so that the two families can be read side by side and translated more easily into code.

  • VDMs are defined by Gaussian distributions. We rewrite each via reparameterisation For example, the forward marginal can be written as $$\begin{aligned} q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x}) &= \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_t ; \; \alpha_t \mathbf{x}, \sigma_t^2 \mathbf{I}\right) && \text{(Gaussian distribution)} \\ \implies \quad \mathbf{z}_t &= \alpha_t \mathbf{x} + \sigma_t \pmb{\epsilon}, \quad \pmb{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}) && \text{(sampling rule)}\end{aligned}$$
  • MDMs are defined by categorical distributions over one-hot vectors. We rewrite these over the clean data values; a Bernoulli variable selects between the clean value \(x_d\) at position \(d\), with probability \(p\), or the mask \(m\), with probability \(1 - p\). For example, the forward marginal can be written as $$\begin{aligned} q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x}) &= \prod_{d=1}^{D} \operatorname{Cat}\left(\mathbf{z}_{t,d} ; \; \alpha_t \mathbf{x}_d + (1 - \alpha_t) \mathbf{m} \right) && \text{(one-hot vectors)} \\ \implies \quad z_{t,d} &= u_d x_d + (1 - u_d) m, \quad u_d \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\alpha_t) && \text{(sampling rule)}\end{aligned}$$

When written like this, both families combine clean data with corruption (either noise or a mask). The original distributions and all derivations are given in the appendix.

In the MDM loss, \(\operatorname{softmax}(\hat{x}_d)_{x_d}\) is the probability of the true value \(x_d\). The remaining terms are

\[\pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}} = \frac{\alpha_{t \mid s} \sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2} \mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\alpha_s \sigma_{t \mid s}^2}{\sigma_t^2} \mathbf{x}, \qquad \sigma^2_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}} = \frac{\sigma_{t \mid s}^2 \sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}, \qquad \rho_{s \mid t} = \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t},\]

with \(\alpha_{t \mid s} = \alpha_t / \alpha_s\) and \(\sigma_{t \mid s}^2 = \sigma_t^2 - \alpha_{t \mid s}^2 \sigma_s^2\).


Interpreting Unmasking

From the forward marginal, recall that \(\alpha_t\) is the retention probability at time \(t\), so \(1 - \alpha_t\) is the fraction of positions that are masked. In the forward posterior, a fraction \(\rho_{s \mid t}\) of the masked positions is revealed when going from time \(t\) to \(s\), so each step reveals an expected fraction \((1 - \alpha_t)\,\rho_{s \mid t} = \alpha_s - \alpha_t\) of positions. Excluding the positions in \(\mathcal{K}\), the expected number of tokens revealed by time \(s\) when sampling is a telescoping sum over the sampling steps,

\[\text{Revealed Tokens at} \; s: \quad \sum_{\text{steps}} (D - |\mathcal{K}|)(\alpha_s - \alpha_t) = (D - |\mathcal{K}|)(\alpha_s - \alpha_1) \approx (D - |\mathcal{K}|)\alpha_s,\]

since \(\alpha_1 \approx 0\). For unconditional generation (\(\mathcal{K} = \emptyset\)), this becomes \(D\alpha_s\).


Remasking Forward Posterior

Once an MDM unmasks a position during sampling, it cannot be changed. ReMDM proposes a forward posterior which can remask visible positions during sampling The ReMDM forward posterior is typically written as $$q_\xi(\mathbf{z}_{s,d} \mid \mathbf{z}_{t,d}, \mathbf{x}_d) = \begin{cases} \operatorname{Cat}\left((1 - \xi_{t,d}) \mathbf{x}_d + \xi_{t,d} \mathbf{m}\right) & \text{if } \mathbf{z}_{t,d} \neq \mathbf{m} \\ \operatorname{Cat}\left(\frac{\alpha_s - (1 - \xi_{t,d}) \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_d + \frac{1 - \alpha_s - \xi_{t,d} \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}\,\mathbf{m}\right) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$ where $0 \leq \xi_{t,d} \leq \min\{1, (1 - \alpha_s)/\alpha_t\}$. For comparison, the standard MDM forward posterior is $$q(\mathbf{z}_{s,d} \mid \mathbf{z}_{t,d}, \mathbf{x}_d) = \begin{cases} \delta_{\mathbf{z}_{t,d}} & \text{if } \mathbf{z}_{t,d} \neq \mathbf{m} \\ \operatorname{Cat}\left(\frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_d + \frac{1 - \alpha_s}{1 - \alpha_t}\,\mathbf{m}\right) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$ Setting $\xi_{t,d} = 0$ recovers this posterior.:

\[z_{s,d} = \begin{cases} u_d z_{t,d} + (1 - u_d) m, \quad u_d \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1 - \xi_{t,d}) & \text{ if } z_{t,d} \neq m, \\ u_d x_d + (1 - u_d) m, \quad u_d \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho^{\xi}_{s \mid t, d}) & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\]

where \(\rho^{\xi}_{s \mid t, d} = (\alpha_s - (1 - \xi_{t,d}) \alpha_t) / (1 - \alpha_t)\) and \(0 \leq \xi_{t,d} \leq \min\{1, (1 - \alpha_s)/\alpha_t\}\). Setting \(\xi_t = 0\) recovers the MDM forward posterior, and the forward marginal remains unchanged. As such, the remasking posterior can be used to sample a pretrained MDM.

The settings of \(\xi_t\) are a design choice. We compare two strategies:

  1. Uniform remasking (ReMDM-unif) uses the same probability \(\xi_t\) for every visible position.
  2. Confidence-based remasking (ReMDM-conf) assigns a per-position probability \(\xi_{t,d}\), favouring positions whose tokens were decoded with low confidence.

Aligning Maths and Code

We now map the maths in the section above onto a simple codebase. Here, code for training, sampling and likelihood-bound estimation can be shared by both diffusion families.

Our framework specifies interfaces for the noise schedule (NoiseSchedule) and diffusion formulation (Diffusion), and a wrapper for the denoiser’s parts (ComposedDenoiser) :

  • NoiseSchedule maps diffusion time \(t\) to a ScheduleState containing parameters which define the corruption level,
  • ComposedDenoiser wraps up a neural network with its input and output adapters for compatibility within VDMs or MDMs,

Training

class Diffusion(ABC):

    def loss(self, x, keep = None, **model_kwargs):
        batch_size = x.size(0)
        # t ~ U[0, 1]
        t = low_discrepancy_timestep_sampling(batch_size)
        t = t.clamp(1e-3, 1.0)
        # S(t) = (nu_t, ...)
        state_t = self.schedule(t)
        # z_t ~ q(z_t | x)
        z_t = self.sample_forward_marginal(x, state_t, keep=keep)
        # x̂ = x̂_θ(z_t, ν_t)
        x_pred = self.predict_clean(z_t, state_t, **model_kwargs)
        # w_t * 𝓔(x̂, x)
        w_t = self.loss_weight(state_t)
        # MDM: score masked positions using z_t; VDM: score the full prediction
        err = self.error(x_pred, x, z_t)
        loss = (w_t * err).mean()
        return loss

Sampling

  class Diffusion(ABC):

    @torch.no_grad()
    def sample_reverse_transition(self, z_t, state_t, state_s, **model_kwargs):
        # x̂ = x̂_θ(z_t, ν_t)
        x_pred = self.predict_clean(z_t, state_t, **model_kwargs)
        # z_s ~ q(z_s | z_t, x = x̂)
        return self.sample_forward_posterior(
            z_t, x_pred, state_t, state_s, **model_kwargs
        )

    @torch.no_grad()
    def sample(self, n_samples, n_steps, terminal_kwargs, model_kwargs):
        model_kwargs = model_kwargs or {}
        # z_1 ~ p(z_1)
        z_t = self.sample_terminal(n_samples, **(terminal_kwargs or {}))
        # ts = [1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2, 0], when n_steps = 5
        ts = torch.linspace(1, 0, n_steps + 1)
        latents = []
        for i in range(n_steps):
            t, s = ts[i].repeat(n_samples), ts[i + 1].repeat(n_samples)
            # (nu_t, ...) = S(t), (nu_s, ....) = S(s)
            state_t, state_s = self.schedule(t), self.schedule(s)
            # z_s ~ p_θ(z_s | z_t)
            z_s = self.sample_reverse_transition(
              z_t, state_t, state_s, **model_kwargs,
            )
            # track all latents
            latents.append(z_s.cpu())
            # reset z_t for the next sampling step
            z_t = z_s
        return z_t, latents

Likelihood-Bound Estimation

  class Diffusion(ABC):

    @torch.no_grad()
    def denoising_bound(self, x, mc_draws, keep = None, **model_kwargs):
        """Return each example's MC mean BPD and unbiased sample variance."""
        assert mc_draws >= 2
        per_example_mean = torch.zeros(x.shape[0], device=x.device)
        per_example_var = torch.zeros(x.shape[0], device=x.device)
        for draw in range(mc_draws):
            # t ~ U[0, 1]
            t = torch.rand(x.shape[0], device=x.device)
            # (nu_t, ...) = S(t)
            state_t = self.schedule(t)
            # z_t ~ q(z_t | x)
            z_t = self.sample_forward_marginal(x, state_t, keep=keep)
            # x̂ = x̂_θ(z_t, ν_t)
            x_pred = self.predict_clean(z_t, state_t, **model_kwargs)
            # MDM: score masked positions using z_t; VDM: score the full prediction
            weighted_error = self.loss_weight(state_t) * self.error(x_pred, x, z_t)
            # Convert nats per dimension to bits per dimension 
            bpd = weighted_error / math.log(2)
            # Update the running mean and variance
            delta = bpd - per_example_mean
            per_example_mean += delta / (draw + 1)
            per_example_var += delta * (bpd - per_example_mean)
        return per_example_mean, per_example_var / (mc_draws - 1)

Closing Thoughts

The difference between continuous and discrete diffusion models can obscure their shared underlying structure, particularly when codebases do not map easily to the equations in papers.

In this post, we closed this gap in three steps:

  1. we organised VDMs and MDMs into three general components - the noise schedule, the denoiser and the diffusion formulation;
  2. we aligned their equations side by side to make these families easier to compare;
  3. we then mapped those equations onto a single codebase shared by both models.

In doing so, our goal was to build a picture of the common components and algorithms shared across diffusion families, so you can focus on the differences that matter instead of getting caught up in implementation details that don’t.

Using our software framework, we also highlight some interesting results:

  • the noise schedule affects image quality more than the denoiser’s architecture (under matched parameters) in both diffusion families;
  • the denoising bound is invariant across noise schedules with the same endpoints, resulting in the same expectation but different variances;
  • an MDM unmasks at the rate dictated by a chosen noise schedule;
  • remasking improves Sudoku solving, with a uniform selection strategy providing most of the gains from a confidence-based one.

We encourage readers to extend our abstraction in their own work on generative modelling. Our setup and design patterns naturally lend themselves to flow-matching , block diffusion , alternative remasking strategies , latent diffusion with VDMs and reasoning tasks . We hope our post makes popular diffusion models easier to understand and lowers the barrier to entry for readers exploring this field.

Notation and conventions

  • Data and latents. \(\mathbf{x}\) is the clean data and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) is its corrupted version at time \(t\in[0,1]\). Time runs from clean to noisy where \(\mathbf{z}_0\) is the least noisy latent and \(\mathbf{z}_1\) is the noisiest.
    • For VDM, \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\in\mathbb{R}^D\).
    • For MDM, \(\mathbf{x}\in\{1,\dots,K-1\}^D\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\in\{1,\dots,K\}^D\) where \(m:= K\) is the mask state.
  • Ordering. Whenever two times appear together, \(s<t\), so \(\mathbf{z}_s\) is less noisy than \(\mathbf{z}_t\)
  • Discretisation. For the lower bound we split \([0,1]\) into \(T\) segments with \(t(i)=i/T\) and \(s(i)=(i-1)/T\) for \(i=1,\dots,T\).

Forward marginal \(q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})\)

The first step is to define how data is corrupted and this is done by prescribing the forward marginal \(q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})\) for \(t\in[0,1]\). This is the distribution of the corrupted state \(\mathbf{z}_t\) given the clean data \(\mathbf{x}\) at time \(t\) where \(t=0\) corresponds to (almost) clean data and \(t=1\) corresponds to the maximally corrupted state. In both cases, the forward process is a continuous-time Markov process, which means that the state at time \(t\) depends only on the state at an earlier time \(s<t\) and not on any other previous states. In either case, we need to design a forward marginal which sufficiently corrupts the data.

VDM

In the case of VDM, the data is modelled as a continuous variable in \(\mathbb{R}^D\). The forward marginal is a Gaussian distribution given by

\[q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_t; \alpha_t\mathbf{x}, \sigma^2_t\mathbf{I})\]

where \(\alpha_t\) and \(\sigma_t\) are the signal and noise coefficients. \(\alpha_t\) and \(\sigma_t\) are functions of time \(t\) which go from 1 to 0 and 0 to 1, respectively. This ensures that at time \(t=0\), the corrupted state \(\mathbf{z}_0\) is equal to the clean data \(\mathbf{x}\), while at time \(t=1\), the corrupted state \(\mathbf{z}_1\) is independent of the clean data and is simply Gaussian noise.

MDM

In the case of MDM, the data, \(\mathbf{x} \in \{1, \dots, K-1\}^D\), is modelled as a discrete variable. The noising process corresponds to masking coordinates of the original data \(\mathbf{x}\). The noising process of MDMs is a continuous-time markov process where at time \(t=0\), the data is clean, and at time \(t=1\) all coordinates are masked. This happens independently for each coordinate. Because of this independence, the forward marginal can be factorised as a product of categorical distributions over each coordinate, given by

\[\begin{aligned} q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}) &= \prod_{d=1}^D q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d)\\ &= \prod_{d=1}^D \left(\alpha_t \delta_{x_d}(z_{t,d}) + (1-\alpha_t) \delta_{m}(z_{t,d})\right)\\ \end{aligned}\]

where \(\delta_{x_d}\) is a Kronecker delta function centred at the clean value \(x_d\), and \(\delta_{m}\) is a Kronecker delta function centred at the mask state. This can be equivalently expressed as a product of categorical distributions over each coordinate, which is what is commonly used in the literature

\[\begin{aligned} q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}) &= \prod_{d=1}^D \operatorname{Cat}\left(z_{t,d}; \alpha_t \mathbf{x}_d + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{m}\right) \end{aligned}\]

where \(\mathbf{x}_d \in \{0,1\}^{K}\) is a one-hot vector corresponding to the clean value \(x_d\), and \(\mathbf{m}\) is a one-hot vector corresponding to the mask state, which we take as the $K$-th state. For example, if \(x_{d} = 4\) then \(\mathbf{x}_{d}\) would be a vector with a 1 in the 4th entry and 0 elsewhere.

Importantly, the combination \(\alpha_t \mathbf{x}_d + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{m} \in [0,1]^{K}\) represents a valid probability distribution over the $K$ categories. For example, if $K=5$, \(\alpha_t = 0.7\), and \(x_d = 2\) then the probability distribution over the categories would be

\[\alpha_t \mathbf{x}_d + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{m} = 0.7 * [0, 1, 0, 0, 0] + 0.3 * [0, 0, 0, 0, 1] = [0, 0.7, 0, 0, 0.3]\]

i.e., at time t, there is a 70% chance the \(d\)th coordinate is the clean value 2, and a 30% chance it is masked.

All together, we see that the probability that the state is \(\mathbf{z}_t\) given the clean data \(\mathbf{x}\) is a product of each of the coordinates being either the clean value or the mask state, with probabilities determined by \(\alpha_t\).

Forward transition \(q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s)\)

From the defined forward marginals, we need to derive transition steps, which give the probability of transitioning from \(\mathbf{z}_s\) at time \(s\) to \(\mathbf{z}_t\) at time \(t\). Next, we can find the forward transition \(q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s)\) for \(0 \leq s < t \leq 1\) as it will be useful later.

VDM

In the case of VDM, the forward marginals are Gaussian, and so the forward transitions, \(q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s)\), are assumed to be Gaussian, following

\[\mathbf{z}_t \stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}, \qquad \pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\]

To find the parameters, \(\alpha_{t\mid s}\) and \(\sigma_{t\mid s}\), of the forward transition, we can use moment matching. As we know that

\[\mathbf{z}_t \stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_t\mathbf{x} + \sigma_t\pmb{\epsilon}_t \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{z}_s \stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_s\mathbf{x} + \sigma_s\pmb{\epsilon}_s\]

we have

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbf{z}_t &\stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\\ &\stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_{t\mid s}(\alpha_s\mathbf{x} + \sigma_s\pmb{\epsilon}_s) + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s} \end{aligned}\]

and so because the forward marginals are Gaussian, we can match the first and second moments to find the parameters, \(\alpha_{t\mid s}\) and \(\sigma_{t\mid s}\). As the first moments are equal, we have

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_t\mid \mathbf{x}] &= \mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\mid \mathbf{x}] \\ &= \alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{x}] + \sigma_{t\mid s}\mathbb{E}[\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\mid \mathbf{x}] \\ &= \alpha_{t\mid s}(\alpha_s\mathbf{x}) + \sigma_{t\mid s}(\mathbf{0}) \\ &= \alpha_{t\mid s}\alpha_s\mathbf{x} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \(\alpha_{t\mid s} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\).

As the second moments are equal, and \(\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\) is independent of \(\mathbf{z}_s\), we have

\[\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}[\mathbf{z}_t\mid \mathbf{x}] &= \operatorname{Var}[\alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\mid \mathbf{x}] \\ &= \alpha_{t\mid s}^2\operatorname{Var}[\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{x}] + \sigma_{t\mid s}^2\operatorname{Var}[\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\mid \mathbf{x}] \\ &= \alpha_{t\mid s}^2(\sigma_s^2\mathbf{I}) + \sigma_{t\mid s}^2(\mathbf{I}) \\ &= (\alpha_{t\mid s}^2\sigma_s^2 + \sigma_{t\mid s}^2)\mathbf{I} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \(\sigma_{t\mid s}^2 = \sigma_t^2 - \alpha_{t\mid s}^2\sigma_s^2 = \sigma_t^2 - \frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^2\)

MDM

In the case of MDMs, we once again use the coordinate-wise independence to decompose the forward transition into a product of coordinate-wise transitions,

\[q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s) = \prod_{d=1}^D q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d})\]

Using this, we can derive the forward transition for each coordinate, \(q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d})\). As we know the marginal is given by \(q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d)\), we need a forward markov transition (i.e. only depends on current state) that is consistent with the marginal. This can be derived using the chapman–kolmogorov equation or law of total probability, which gives

\[\begin{aligned} q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d) &= \sum_{z_{s,d}} q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d}, x_d)\,q(z_{s,d}\mid x_d)\\ &= \sum_{z_{s,d}} q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d})\,q(z_{s,d}\mid x_d) \end{aligned}\]

This equation is pretty much saying that the probability of a coordinate being in a certain state at time \(t\), given the input, is the sum of the probabilities of all possible transitions from time \(s\) to time \(t\), weighted by the probability of the intermediate state. However, as the forward transition is markov, we can drop the dependency on \(x_d\) in the first term. Additionally, because we know that the only possible values for the forward marginal given \(x_d\) are \(x_d\) and the mask state \(\mathbf{m}\), we can simplify the sum to only include these two states, giving

\[\begin{aligned} q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d) = q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d) + q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d}=m)\,q(z_{s,d}=m\mid x_d) \end{aligned}\]

When considering the case where \(z_{t,d} = x_d\), we have,

\[\begin{aligned} q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid x_d) &= \alpha_t \\ &= q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d) + q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=m)\,q(z_{s,d}=m\mid x_d)\\ &= q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,\alpha_s + q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=m)\,(1-\alpha_s)\\ &= q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,\alpha_s + 0\cdot(1-\alpha_s)\\ &= q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,\alpha_s \end{aligned}\]

where we use the defined marginals to get \(q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d) = \alpha_s, q(z_{s,d}=m\mid x_d) = 1-\alpha_s\) and \(q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid x_d) = \alpha_{t}\) and also the fact that if \(z_{s,d}\) is masked, then \(z_{t,d}\) cannot be the clean value \(x_d\), so \(q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=m) = 0\).

Denoting \(q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d) = \alpha_{t\mid s}\), we have

\[\alpha_t = \alpha_{t\mid s}\,\alpha_s \implies \alpha_{t\mid s} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\]

So the probability that a coordinate is the clean value at time \(t\) given that it was the clean value at time \(s\) is the ratio of the probabilities of being the clean value at time \(t\) and time \(s\).

Forward posterior \(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\)

Another useful quantity is the forward posterior, \(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\) for \(0 \leq s < t \leq 1\), which can be derived using the conditional Bayes’ rule and the markov property as follows

\[q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \frac{q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s)\,q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}\]

VDM

In the case of VDM, the forward marginals and forward transitions are Gaussian, so \(\mathbf{z}_s\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) are jointly Gaussian given \(\mathbf{x}\), and hence the forward posterior is also Gaussian,

\[q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_s;\ \pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x},\ \pmb{\Sigma}_{s\mid t,x})\]

The mean and covariance follow from the standard identities for conditioning a multivariate Gaussian (Section 2.3.1 of ):

\[\begin{aligned} \pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x} &= \pmb{\mu}_s + \pmb{\Sigma}_{s,t}\pmb{\Sigma}_t^{-1}(\mathbf{z}_t - \pmb{\mu}_t) \\ \pmb{\Sigma}_{s\mid t,x} &= \pmb{\Sigma}_s - \pmb{\Sigma}_{s,t}\pmb{\Sigma}_t^{-1}\pmb{\Sigma}_{t,s} \end{aligned}\]

where, given \(\mathbf{x}\), the moments of the forward marginals are

\[\pmb{\mu}_s = \alpha_s\mathbf{x}, \qquad \pmb{\mu}_t = \alpha_t\mathbf{x}, \qquad \pmb{\Sigma}_s = \sigma_s^2\mathbf{I}, \qquad \pmb{\Sigma}_t = \sigma_t^2\mathbf{I}\]

For the cross-covariance, write \(\mathbf{z}_t = \alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\) with \(\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\) independent of \(\mathbf{z}_s\). Then

\[\pmb{\Sigma}_{s,t} = \operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{z}_s,\mathbf{z}_t] = \alpha_{t\mid s}\operatorname{Var}[\mathbf{z}_s] = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\sigma_s^2\mathbf{I}\]

Substituting these in, we get the mean and covariance of the forward posterior,

\[\begin{aligned} \pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x} &= \alpha_s\mathbf{x} + \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\sigma_s^2\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_t^2}\left(\mathbf{z}_t - \alpha_t\mathbf{x}\right) \\[6pt] &= \frac{\alpha_t\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \alpha_s\left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2}\right)\mathbf{x} \\[6pt] &= \frac{\alpha_t\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2 - \alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{x} \end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned} \pmb{\Sigma}_{s\mid t,x} &= \sigma_s^2\mathbf{I} - \frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^4\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_t^2}\mathbf{I} \\[6pt] &= \frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}\left(\sigma_t^2 - \frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^2\right)\mathbf{I} \\[6pt] &= \frac{\sigma_s^2\left(\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2 - \alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2\right)}{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{I} \end{aligned}\]

Therefore

\[q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_s;\ \frac{\alpha_t\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2 - \alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{x},\ \frac{\sigma_s^2\left(\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2 - \alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2\right)}{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{I}\right)\]

Using \(\alpha_{t\mid s}=\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\) and \(\sigma^2_{t\mid s}=\sigma_t^2-\frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^2\) from the transition, this can be written more compactly as

\[q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_s;\ \frac{\alpha_{t\mid s}\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\alpha_s\sigma^2_{t\mid s}}{\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{x},\ \frac{\sigma^2_{t\mid s}\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}\mathbf{I}\right)\]

MDM

For the case of MDM, we use the coordinate wise independence to factorise the forward posterior, which is given by

\[q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{d=1}^D q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\]

Then we can apply the conditional Bayes’ rule to each coordinate, giving

\[q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d) = \frac{q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d})\,q(z_{s,d}\mid x_d)}{q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d)}\]

In words, this probability gives the probability that a coordinate is in a certain state at an earlier time \(s\), given that it is in a certain state at a later time \(t\) and the clean data.

Then to figure out what the forward posterior is, we can consider the possible values of \(z_{t,d}\) because \(z_{t,d}\) can only be either the clean value \(x_d\) (\(x_d\) cannot be masked by assumption) or the mask state \(\mathbf{m}\). The cases are as follows:

  • Unmasked later, \(z_{t,d} = x_d\) and masked earlier, \(z_{s,d} = \mathbf{m}\)
  • Unmasked later, \(z_{t,d} = x_d\) and unmasked earlier, \(z_{s,d} = x_d\)
  • Masked later, \(z_{t,d} = \mathbf{m}\) and unmasked earlier, \(z_{s,d} = x_d\)
  • Masked later, \(z_{t,d} = \mathbf{m}\) and masked earlier, \(z_{s,d} = \mathbf{m}\)

When \(z_{t,d} = x_d\), it means that the coordinate is unmasked at time \(t\), and so it must have been unmasked at time \(s\). Therefore, we have

\[q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=x_d, x_d) = 1\] \[q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=x_d, x_d) = 0\]

When the coordinate is masked at time \(t\), it could have been either masked or unmasked at time \(s\). Therefore, we have

  • \(q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{s,d}=x_d) = 1-\alpha_{t\mid s}\), which is the probability that a coordinate is masked at time \(t\) given that it was the clean value at time \(s\).
  • \(q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}) = 1\), which is the probability that a coordinate is masked at time \(t\) given that it was masked at time \(s\).
  • \(q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid x_d) = 1-\alpha_t\), which is the probability that a coordinate is masked at time \(t\) given the clean data.
  • \(q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d) = \alpha_s\), which is the probability that a coordinate is the clean value at time \(s\) given the clean data.

Then we can use the conditional Bayes’ rule to get

\[\begin{aligned} &q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\\ &= \frac{q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d)}{q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid x_d)}\\ &= \frac{(1-\alpha_{t\mid s})\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\\ &= \frac{(1-\alpha_t/\alpha_s)\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\\ &= \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t} := \rho_{s\mid t} \end{aligned}\]

and

\[\begin{aligned} &q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\\ &= \frac{q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m})\,q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid x_d)}{q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid x_d)}\\ &= \frac{1\cdot(1-\alpha_s)}{1-\alpha_t}\\ &= \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t} = 1 - \rho_{s\mid t} \end{aligned}\]

We have now considered all the possible cases for \(z_{t,d}\) and \(z_{s,d}\), and so we have derived the forward posterior for MDMs. In summary, we have

\[\begin{aligned} q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d) =\begin{cases} \delta_{x_d}(z_{s,d}) & \text{if } z_{t,d} = x_d\\ \rho_{s\mid t}\,\delta_{x_d}(z_{s,d}) + (1-\rho_{s\mid t})\,\delta_{\mathbf{m}}(z_{s,d}) & \text{if } z_{t,d} = \mathbf{m} \end{cases} \end{aligned}\]

Then the forward posterior for the whole state is given by

\[q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{d=1}^D q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\]

Variational Lower Bound

Following VDM, time is continuous on \([0,1]\), with \(\mathbf{z}_0\) the least noisy latent and \(\mathbf{z}_1\) the noisiest. We discretise into \(T\) segments with \(t(i)=i/T\) and \(s(i)=(i-1)/T\) for \(i=1,\dots,T\), so each step goes from \(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\) to \(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\). We write \(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\) for all \(T+1\) latents.

As we want to maximise the log-likelihood of the data, we can use the variational lower bound to derive a loss function. We can write the log-likelihood as

\[\begin{aligned} \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) &= \log \int p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{0:1})\,d\mathbf{z}_{0:1} \\ &= \log \int \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{0:1})}{q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})} q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{z}_{0:1} \end{aligned}\]

Using the following factorisation of the \(p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{0:1})\) and \(q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})\), we have

\[\begin{aligned} p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{0:1}) &= p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(1)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(1)})\cdots p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(T)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(T)})p(\mathbf{z}_1)\\ &= p(\mathbf{z}_1)\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\prod_{i=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\\ q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x}) &= q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})q(\mathbf{z}_{s(T)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(T)},\mathbf{x})\cdots q(\mathbf{z}_{s(1)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(1)},\mathbf{x})\\ &= q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^T q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x}) \end{aligned}\]

we get, using the compact product form, that

\[\begin{aligned} \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) &= \log \int \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\prod_{i=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^T q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})} q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\\ &= \log \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\prod_{i=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^T q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})}\right] \\ &\geq \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\prod_{i=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^T q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})}\right] =: \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}) \end{aligned}\]

where the inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality (\(\log \mathbb{E}[f(X)] \geq \mathbb{E}[\log f(X)]\)). We can further simplify the pointwise variational lower bound, \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})\), as follows

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0) + \sum_{i=1}^{T} \log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})} + \log \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})}\right]\\ &=\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_0\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\right] + \sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)},\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})}\right] + \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})}\right]\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_0\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\right] - \sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\right)\right] - D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p(\mathbf{z}_1)\right) \end{aligned}\]

In the second equality, we see that the terms in the sum are only dependent on \(z_{s(i)},z_{t(i)}\) given \(x\), so the expectation over all \(z_{0:1}\) is simplified to an expectation over \(z_{s(i)},z_{t(i)}\) given \(x\). In the third equality, we split the expectation \(\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{s(i)},z_{t(i)}\mid x)} = \mathbb{E}_{q(z_{t(i)}\mid x)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{s(i)}\mid z_{t(i)},x)}\right]\) and use the following definition of the KL divergence

\[D_{KL}(P\,\|\,Q) = \int P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} dx\]

VDM

To resolve the KL terms, we need to choose the model \(p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\). The forward posterior \(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\) is the Gaussian derived above, and it depends on the clean data \(\mathbf{x}\), which the model doesn’t have at sampling time. So we use the same Gaussian with \(\mathbf{x}\) replaced by a prediction \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)\) from a neural network

\[p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t) = q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)) = \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_s;\ \pmb{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t),\ \sigma^2_{s\mid t,x}\mathbf{I}\right)\]

Both Gaussians in the KL then have the same covariance, \(\sigma^2_{s\mid t,x}\mathbf{I}\), and differ only in their means. For two Gaussians with equal covariance, the trace term equals $D$ and the log-determinant term is zero, so the KL reduces to

\[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2_{s\mid t,x}}\left\|\pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x} - \pmb{\mu}_\theta\right\|^2\]

The means differ only in the coefficient on \(\mathbf{x}\) (the \(\mathbf{z}_t\) terms cancel)

\[\pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x} - \pmb{\mu}_\theta = \frac{\alpha_s\sigma^2_{t\mid s}}{\sigma_t^2}\left(\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta\right), \qquad \sigma^2_{s\mid t,x} = \frac{\sigma^2_{t\mid s}\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}\]

Substituting,

\[D_{KL} = \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2\sigma^2_{t\mid s}\sigma_s^2}\cdot\frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma^4_{t\mid s}}{\sigma_t^4}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta\right\|^2 = \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma^2_{t\mid s}}{2\sigma_s^2\sigma_t^2}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta\right\|^2\]

With \(\mathrm{SNR}(t)=\alpha_t^2/\sigma_t^2\) and \(\sigma^2_{t\mid s}=\sigma_t^2-\frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^2\), we have

\[\mathrm{SNR}(s)-\mathrm{SNR}(t) = \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2-\alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2\sigma_t^2} = \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma^2_{t\mid s}}{\sigma_s^2\sigma_t^2}\]

so each diffusion loss term is a weighted MSE

\[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{SNR}(s)-\mathrm{SNR}(t)\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)\right\|^2\]

MDM

As done in the VDM case, the model for MDM is given by the forward posterior with \(\mathbf{x}\) replaced by a prediction \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)\) from a neural network which outputs a distribution over the possible values for each coordinate of \(\mathbf{x}\). The forward posterior factorises over coordinates, so we can also factorise the model

\[\begin{aligned} p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t) &= \prod_{d=1}^D p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}) \end{aligned}\]

where

\[\begin{aligned} p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})= \begin{cases} \delta_{z_{t,d}}(z_{s,d}) & z_{t,d}\neq\mathbf m\\[4pt] \left(1 - \rho_{s\mid t}\right)\,\delta_{\mathbf m}(z_{s,d})+\,\rho_{s\mid t}\,\hat x_{\theta,d}(z_{s,d};\mathbf z_t,\nu_t) & z_{t,d}=\mathbf m \end{cases} \end{aligned}\]

where \(\hat{x}_{\theta,d}(z_{s,d};\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t)\) is the probability that the \(d\)th coordinate of \(\mathbf{x}\) is \(z_{s,d}\) given the latent \(\mathbf{z}_t\) and noise level $\nu_t$, as predicted by the neural network.

We first consider a KL term for a timestep which is given by

\[\begin{aligned} &D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right) \\ &= \sum_{d=1}^D D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\right) \end{aligned}\]

Next, we consider the term in the sum for a single coordinate,

\[\begin{aligned} &D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\right) \end{aligned}\]

Using the definition of KL, \(D_{KL}(P\,\|\,Q) = \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\), we can write this as

\[\begin{aligned} &D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\right)\\ &= \sum_{z_{s, d} \in \{x_d, \mathbf{m}\}} q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\log\frac{q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)}{p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})} \end{aligned}\]

If \(z_{t,d} = x_d\) is unmasked, the KL term is zero because the two distributions, \(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\) and \(p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\), are identical as \(z_{s,d}\) is always going to be \(x_d\). However, if \(z_{t,d} = \mathbf{m}\) is masked, then \(z_{s,d}\) can be either \(x_d\) or \(\mathbf{m}\), and the KL term is non-zero.

\[\begin{aligned} &D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m})\right)\\ &= \sum_{z_{s, d} \in \{x_d, \mathbf{m}\}} q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\log\frac{q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)}{p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m})}\\ &= q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\log\frac{q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)}{p_\theta(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m})}\\ &\qquad+ q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\log\frac{q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)}{p_\theta(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m})}\\ &= \rho_{s\mid t}\log\frac{\rho_{s\mid t}}{\rho_{s \mid t}\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,\nu_t)} + (1-\rho_{s\mid t})\log\frac{1-\rho_{s\mid t}}{1-\rho_{s \mid t}}\\ &= -\rho_{s\mid t}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,\nu_t) \end{aligned}\]

Combining the two cases, we can write the KL term for a single coordinate as

\[D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\right) = \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t,d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[-\rho_{s\mid t}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,\nu_t)\right]\]

Next, combining the KL terms for all coordinates, we have

\[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t,d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[-\rho_{s\mid t}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,\nu_t)\right]\]

Continuous Time Limit

In this section, we discuss how we can go from a discrete time implementation of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}\) seen in the preliminary sections to a continuous time implementation. The discrete diffusion loss is given below, and it can be estimated using the given unbiased estimator

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\right)\right]\\ &= T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \left[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\right)\right]\\ \end{aligned}\]

It is shown in the VDM paper (Appendix F of ) that a finer discretisation of the diffusion process is better because doing so makes the variational lower bound closer to the true log-likelihood. This is good because we ideally want to optimise the true log-likelihood, but we can only optimise the variational lower bound. Therefore, if we get closer to the true log-likelihood, we are optimising an objective closer to what we want.

Thus, we see what happens when we take the limit as \(T\to\infty\). This would mean making the grid finer, and while the number of terms in the sum increases, the gap between \(s\) and \(t\) becomes very small. This is done for both VDM and MDM using the KL terms derived above.

VDM

The main strategy to see it is to carefully use our definitons of \(s(i)\) and \(t(i)\) to rewrite the difference in SNR terms as a finite difference, which becomes a derivative in the limit. We have

\[\begin{aligned} \lim_{T \to \infty} \mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}} &= \lim_{T \to \infty} T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{SNR}(s(i))-\mathrm{SNR}(t(i))\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\nu_{t(i)})\right\|^2\\ &= \lim_{T \to \infty} T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{SNR}(t(i-1))-\mathrm{SNR}(t(i))\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\nu_{t(i)})\right\|^2\\ &= \lim_{T \to \infty} \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{\mathrm{SNR}(t(i))-\mathrm{SNR}(t(i-1))}{1/T}\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\nu_{t(i)})\right\|^2\\ &= \lim_{T \to \infty} \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{\mathrm{SNR}(t(i))-\mathrm{SNR}(t(i-1))}{t(i)-t(i-1)}\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\nu_{t(i)})\right\|^2\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z}_t\sim q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(-\mathrm{SNR}'(t)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)\right\|^2\right) \end{aligned}\]

MDM

In the case of MDM, we do exactly the same, but use a different KL term. We have

\[\begin{aligned} \lim_{T \to \infty} \mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}} &= \lim_{T \to \infty} T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t(i),d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[-\frac{\alpha_{s(i)}-\alpha_{t(i)}}{1-\alpha_{t(i)}}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_{t(i)},\nu_{t(i)})\right]\\ &= \lim_{T \to \infty} T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t(i),d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[-\frac{\alpha_{t(i-1)}-\alpha_{t(i)}}{1-\alpha_{t(i)}}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_{t(i)},\nu_{t(i)})\right]\\ &= \lim_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t(i),d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{t(i)}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t(i)}-\alpha_{t(i-1)}}{1/T}\right)\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_{t(i)},\nu_{t(i)})\right]\\ &= \lim_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t(i),d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{t(i)}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t(i)}-\alpha_{t(i-1)}}{t(i)-t(i-1)}\right)\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_{t(i)},\nu_{t(i)})\right]\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z}_t\sim q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}} \left[\frac{\alpha_t'}{1-\alpha_t}\sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t,d}=\mathbf m\}}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,\nu_t)\right] \end{aligned}\]

We see in the continous time limits, the weights, \(\omega(t)\), for the loss terms are given by the derivatives of the SNR and \(\alpha\) functions.
