## Introduction

Masked diffusion models (MDMs) <d-cite key="sahoo2024simple"></d-cite><d-cite key="austin2021structured"></d-cite><d-cite key="shi2024simplified"></d-cite> have recently gained popularity for their ability to generate text in parallel instead of one token at a time <d-cite key="labs2025mercury"></d-cite><d-cite key="nie2026large"></d-cite><d-cite key="team2026diffusiongemma"></d-cite>.
Like the variational diffusion models (VDMs) widely used for images, MDMs learn to generate data by reversing a corruption process. In the case of VDMs, the corruption and generation are Gaussian, whereas for MDMs, these processes are discrete via masking.
If you first learned about diffusion through image generation via VDMs <d-cite key="kingma2021variational"></d-cite> or denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) <d-cite key="ho2020denoising"></d-cite>, learning about their discrete counterparts can initially feel very alien.<d-footnote>We have chosen our perspective with respect to VDMs instead of DDPMs for the following reasons: 1. VDMs have inherently better performance than DDPMs by training in continuous rather than discrete time. 2. When comparing the papers, while the objectives are the same, the VDM formulation can be derived more easily, and we believe it is an easier framework to reason about than DDPMs. 3. Many advancements have built on top of VDM (ELBO with data augmentation, Simple Diffusion 1 and 2, Diffusion LLMs).</d-footnote>

When we get stuck on a paper, some of us may turn to the code for clarity.
Matching an equation to a few lines of Python, or spotting a connection to an implementation we already know, often helps things click. 
Yet discrete diffusion codebases can introduce another layer of unfamiliarity — different method names, different implementation choices, and seemingly fewer conceptual connections to the DDPM <d-cite key="ho2020denoising"></d-cite>, DiT <d-cite key="peebles2023scalable"></d-cite> and SiT <d-cite key="ma2024sit"></d-cite> codebases we know and love.
Others may prefer to get their heads around the maths first. However, connecting this back to software isn’t always straightforward, as naming conventions may not correspond to equations and implementations may rely on implicit mathematical identities.

In this post, we’ll work through VDMs and MDMs side by side, connecting their equations to a shared implementation.
Specifically, we outline a software abstraction which separates these models into three components: a noise schedule, a denoiser and the core diffusion formulation.
This means we can disentangle what each component controls, how they interact, and what can be changed independently.
Additionally, our abstraction enables shared code for training, sampling and likelihood-bound estimation across both diffusion families.
Through image generation, text generation and Sudoku solving, we investigate theoretical properties and show which components can be reused across diffusion families and tasks.
We hope this helps to build a mental model that aligns the maths with the code, which you can carry into the next paper you read.

## Preliminaries

Diffusion models share the same components: a noise schedule, a denoiser and the core diffusion formulation.
The schedule specifies the corruption level, the denoiser predicts clean data, and the model formulation defines the corruption distributions, generative transitions, the objective and a bound on the data likelihood.
We introduce these components at a high level here and provide definitions for VDMs and MDMs in the next section.
Full derivations are in the appendix.

Let $$\mathbf{x}$$ denote clean data and $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ a corrupted state at time $$t\in[0,1]$$.
Corruption, via the fixed *forward* process, proceeds from $$t = 0$$ to $$t=1$$, following the Markov chain 

$$
\mathbf{x} \rightarrow \mathbf{z}_0 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow \mathbf{z}_1
$$

while generation, via the learned *reverse* process, occurs in the opposite direction,

$$
\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{z}_0 \leftarrow \cdots \leftarrow \mathbf{z}_1
$$

We use $$q(\cdot)$$ for forward distributions and $$p_\theta(\cdot)$$ for the learned reverse distributions.

### Noise Schedule
The noise schedule $$\mathcal{S}(\cdot)$$ maps time $$t$$ to a set of parameters describing the corruption level,

$$
\mathcal{S}(t) = \{\nu_t, \ldots\},
$$

which parameterise the forward distributions.
All schedules return $$\nu_t$$, derived from the other schedule parameters, which describes the corruption level at time $$t$$.
Here its endpoints at $$t = 0$$ and $$t = 1$$ are denoted $$\nu_{\text{min}}$$ and $$\nu_{\text{max}}$$, respectively. 
The time $$t$$ should be interpreted as an arbitrary coordinate along the diffusion path, while the noise schedule determines the corruption level at $$t$$.
Hence, two schedules may map the same $$t$$ to different corruption levels, i.e. $$\mathcal{S}_1(t) \neq \mathcal{S}_2(t)$$.

### Denoiser
A denoising neural network estimates clean data from a corrupted state at time $$t$$,

$$
\hat{\textbf{x}} = \hat{\textbf{x}}_\theta(\textbf{z}_t, \nu_t),
$$

where $$\nu_t$$ is provided by the noise schedule.
In many works, the denoiser is conditioned directly on time $$t$$ as opposed to $$\nu_t$$ <d-cite key="ho2020denoising"></d-cite><d-cite key="peebles2023scalable"></d-cite><d-cite key="ma2024sit"></d-cite>.
However, $$t$$ may index noise levels differently for each noise schedule.
By conditioning on $$\nu_t$$, our denoiser is given the same conditioning values for the same corruption level, regardless of the noise schedule.
This design choice will help us understand properties of likelihood-bound estimation.
The denoiser is parameterised by a transformer or U-Net, with input and output adapters to ensure that data is compatible with the network and $$\mathbf{x}$$-predictions are compatible with the loss function, respectively.

### Diffusion Formulation
The diffusion formulation specifies how data are corrupted and how the denoiser’s predictions define reverse transitions.
Together with the noise schedule and denoiser, these definitions give us common procedures for training, sampling and likelihood-bound estimation.

#### Corruption in the Forward Direction
The forward marginal defines the distribution over a corrupted state $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ given clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$,

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x}).
$$

At $$t = 1$$, where we have the most corruption, the marginal is no longer dependent on $$\mathbf{x}$$, $$q(\mathbf{z}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) \approx q(\mathbf{z}_1)$$.
The forward marginal lets us sample $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ directly from $$\mathbf{x}$$. To sample between *any* two timesteps $$s < t$$, we define two additional forward distributions:
- the forward transition $$q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{z}_s)$$ lets us sample a more corrupted $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ from a given $$\mathbf{z}_s$$,
- and the forward posterior $$q(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x})$$ lets us sample a less corrupted $$\mathbf{z}_s$$ given a more corrupted $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ and corresponding clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$.

#### Generation in the Reverse Direction

We generate new data by ancestral sampling.<d-footnote>Akin to DDPM, however, as VDMs are trained in continuous time, our steps need not be defined via the same discrete steps as DDPM.<d-footnote>
This begins by sampling a simple terminal distribution $$p(\mathbf{z}_1) = q(\mathbf{z}_1)$$ and then sampling the learned reverse transitions $$p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t)$$ over a sequence of decreasing diffusion times $$1 = t_N > t_{N-1} > \cdots > t_1 > t_0 = 0$$,

$$
\mathbf{z}_1 \sim p(\mathbf{z}_1) = q(\mathbf{z}_1), \qquad \mathbf{z}_s \sim p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t), \qquad 0 \leq s < t \leq 1.
$$

The reverse transitions are defined in terms of the forward posterior.
If the clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$ were known, the forward posterior defines how to move from $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ to a less noisy state $$\mathbf{z}_s$$.
For generation, we instead estimate $$\mathbf{x}$$ from the current state $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ using a neural network $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\cdot)$$,

$$
p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t) = q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)).
$$

#### Training

We train the generative model by minimising a negative evidence lower bound (NELBO),

$$
-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \leq \text{NELBO}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diffusion}},
$$

with each term defined as follows,

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}} = D_\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(\mathbf{z}_1 \mid \mathbf{x})\,\Vert\,p(\mathbf{z}_1)\right), \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} = \mathrm{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_0 \mid \mathbf{x})}\left[-\log p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z}_0)\right], \\
\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}} =
\sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathrm{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)} \mid \mathbf{x})} \left[D_\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)} \mid \mathbf{z}_{t(i)}, \mathbf{x}) \,\Vert\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)} \mid \mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\right)\right],
\end{gathered}
$$

where $$t(i) = i / T$$ and $$s(i) = (i - 1)/T$$ denote two adjacent timesteps, with $$s(i) < t(i)$$ and step index $$i \in \{1, \cdots, T\}$$.
The $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}$$ and $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$$ are generally negligible.
In the limit $$T \rightarrow \infty$$, the remaining $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}$$ term has a common form in VDMs and MDMs,

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diffusion}}(\mathbf{x}) 
&= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z}_t\sim q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}}
\left[\omega(t)\,\mathcal{E}\!\left(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)\right)\right] 
% &= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z}_t\sim q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}} \left[\tilde{\omega}(t)\,\mathcal{E}\!\left(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\tilde{\nu_t})\right)\right]
% &= \int^{1}_{0} \omega(t) \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_t \sim q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x})} \big[ \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t)) \big] dt\\
% &= \int^{1}_{0} f'(t) \omega(\mathcal{V}(t)) \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_{\nu} \sim q(\mathbf{z}_{\nu} \mid \mathbf{x})} \big[ \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_\nu, f(\nu_t))) \big] dt
\end{aligned}
$$

where $$\mathcal{E}(\cdot)$$ measures prediction error, $$\omega(t)$$ weights its contribution. 
If we want to use different noise schedules, we can use the change of variables formula, to adjust the weighting $$\omega(t)$$ and preserve $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}$$.

Training across VDMs and MDMs therefore follows a common recipe:

1. Sample a time via $$t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)$$
2. Corrupt the clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$ via $$\mathbf{z}_t \sim q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x})$$
3. Predict the clean data $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}$$ using the neural network $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t)$$
4. Compute a weighted error between the predicted and clean data $$\omega(t) \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}})$$.

#### Likelihood Estimation

We can estimate the likelihood of the data under the model by using Monte Carlo to estimate of the following,

$$
\mathbb{E}_\mathbf{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z}_t\sim q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}}
\left[\omega(t)\,\mathcal{E}\!\left(\mathbf{x},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,\nu_t)\right)\right]\right].
$$

Once a model is trained with a specific noise schedule, the likelihood can be estimated with different schedules. However, while the expectation of the likelihood bound is invariant to the noise schedule, the variance of the estimate can change. This means we have to find a noise schedule which reduces variance of the estimator.


## A Simple Recipe for VDMs and MDMs
The previous section describes the distributions and entities that need to be defined to build a diffusion model.
We now make this concrete for VDMs and MDMs.
We first present a software framework with interfaces for the model formulation, noise schedule and denoiser.
We then outline how our framework implements VDMs and MDMs, by linking maths to code.
Notably, algorithms for training, sampling and estimating the likelihood-bound from learned components are shared across both diffusion families.

### Software Interface and Shared Algorithms
Our software framework uses interfaces, via [abstract base classes](https://docs.python.org/3/library/abc.html), for the noise schedule (`NoiseSchedule`), denoiser (`ComposedDenoiser`) and the diffusion formulation (`Diffusion`):
- `NoiseSchedule` returns a `ScheduleState` containing the parameters of the corresponding corruption level, including $$\nu_t$$ on which the denoiser is conditioned.
- `ComposedDenoiser` wraps up a neural network with input and output adapters for compatibility within  VDMs or MDMs.
- `Diffusion` [composes](https://realpython.com/inheritance-composition-python/) the noise schedule and denoiser to implement generic methods for the loss function, ancestral sampler and likelihood estimation from the learned diffusion components. **These algorithms are shared by VDM and MDM subclasses.**

#### Training 

```python
class Diffusion(ABC):

    def loss(self, x, keep = None, **model_kwargs):
        batch_size = x.size(0)
        # t ~ U[0, 1]
        t = low_discrepancy_timestep_sampling(batch_size)
        t = t.clamp(1e-3, 1.0)
        # S(t) = (nu_t, ...)
        state_t = self.schedule(t)
        # z_t ~ q(z_t | x)
        z_t = self.sample_forward_marginal(x, state_t, keep=keep)
        # x̂ = x̂_θ(z_t, ν_t)
        x_pred = self.predict_clean(z_t, state_t, **model_kwargs)
        # w_t * 𝓔(x̂, x)
        w_t = self.loss_weight(state_t)
        # MDM: score masked positions using z_t; VDM: score the full prediction
        err = self.error(x_pred, x, z_t)
        loss = (w_t * err).mean()
        return loss
```

#### Sampling 

```python
  class Diffusion(ABC):

    @torch.no_grad()
    def sample_reverse_transition(self, z_t, state_t, state_s, **model_kwargs):
        # x̂ = x̂_θ(z_t, ν_t)
        x_pred = self.predict_clean(z_t, state_t, **model_kwargs)
        # z_s ~ q(z_s | z_t, x = x̂)
        return self.sample_forward_posterior(
            z_t, x_pred, state_t, state_s, **model_kwargs
        )

    @torch.no_grad()
    def sample(self, n_samples, n_steps, terminal_kwargs, model_kwargs):
        model_kwargs = model_kwargs or {}
        # z_1 ~ p(z_1)
        z_t = self.sample_terminal(n_samples, **(terminal_kwargs or {}))
        # ts = [1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2, 0], when n_steps = 5
        ts = torch.linspace(1, 0, n_steps + 1)
        latents = []
        for i in range(n_steps):
            t, s = ts[i].repeat(n_samples), ts[i + 1].repeat(n_samples)
            # (nu_t, ...) = S(t), (nu_s, ....) = S(s)
            state_t, state_s = self.schedule(t), self.schedule(s)
            # z_s ~ p_θ(z_s | z_t)
            z_s = self.sample_reverse_transition(
              z_t, state_t, state_s, **model_kwargs,
            )
            # track all latents
            latents.append(z_s.cpu())
            # reset z_t for the next sampling step
            z_t = z_s
        return z_t, latents
```

#### Likelihood-Bound Estimation

```python
  class Diffusion(ABC):

    @torch.no_grad()
    def denoising_bpd(self, x, mc_draws, keep = None, **model_kwargs):
        """Return each example's MC mean BPD and unbiased sample variance."""
        assert mc_draws >= 2
        per_example_mean = torch.zeros(x.shape[0], device=x.device)
        per_example_var = torch.zeros(x.shape[0], device=x.device)
        for draw in range(mc_draws):
            # t ~ U[0, 1]
            t = torch.rand(x.shape[0], device=x.device)
            # (nu_t, ...) = S(t)
            state_t = self.schedule(t)
            # z_t ~ q(z_t | x)
            z_t = self.sample_forward_marginal(x, state_t, keep=keep)
            # x̂ = x̂_θ(z_t, ν_t)
            x_pred = self.predict_clean(z_t, state_t, **model_kwargs)
            # MDM: score masked positions using z_t; VDM: score the full prediction
            weighted_error = self.loss_weight(state_t) * self.error(x_pred, x, z_t)
            # Convert nats per dimension to bits per dimension 
            bpd = weighted_error / math.log(2)
            # Update the running mean and variance
            delta = bpd - per_example_mean
            per_example_mean += delta / (draw + 1)
            per_example_var += delta * (bpd - per_example_mean)
        return per_example_mean, per_example_var / (mc_draws - 1)
```

### Noise Schedule Implementation
{: #noise-schedule-implementation }
A noise schedule, implemented under the `NoiseSchedule` interface, maps $$t$$ to a `ScheduleState` $$\mathcal{S}(t)$$, containing corruption parameters and $$\nu_t$$ for conditioning the denoiser.
Noise schedules specific to VDMs or MDMs have shared components with different interpretations.
<!--We subclass this interface through `VPSchedule(NoiseSchedule)` for VDMs and `MaskSchedule(NoiseSchedule)` for MDMs. -->
The table below provides a comparison.

|  | VDM <br>`VPSchedule(NoiseSchedule)` | MDM <br>`MaskSchedule(NoiseSchedule)` |
| :-- | :--: | :--: |
| **Schedule State $$\mathcal{S}(t)$$** | $$(\alpha_t,\sigma_t,\gamma_t,\nu_t)$$ | $$(\alpha_t,\gamma_t,\nu_t)$$ |
| **Defn. of $$\alpha_t$$** | $$\sqrt{\text{sigmoid}(\gamma_t)}$$ <br> clean signal coefficient | Specified by the noise schedule - <br> clean token retention probability |
| **Defn. of $$\sigma_t$$** | $$\sqrt{\text{sigmoid}(-\gamma_t)}$$ <br> noise signal coefficient | Not applicable |
| **Defn. of $$\gamma_t$$** | $$\log\frac{\alpha^2_t}{\sigma^2_t}=\log\operatorname{SNR}(t)$$ <br> log signal-to-noise ratio | $$\log(1-\alpha_t)$$ <br> log masking probability |
| **Conditioning value $$\nu_t$$**    | $$\gamma_t$$ | $$-\log\alpha_t$$ |

VDM schedules must specify $$\gamma_t$$ in order to compute the signal $$\alpha_t$$ and noise $$\sigma_t$$ coefficients<d-footnote>These coefficients satisfy the variance-preserving (VP) constraint, $\alpha_t^2 + \sigma_t^2 = 1$. All VDMs considered here use variance-preserving schedules. Other Gaussian corruption processes can be variance-exploding or sub-VP <d-cite key="kingma2023understanding"></d-cite><d-cite key="song2020score"></d-cite>.</d-footnote>.
The log-SNR goes from $$\gamma_\max$$ at $$t = 0$$ to $$\gamma_\min$$ at $$t = 1$$.

Within each diffusion family, different schedules can map the same time $$t$$ to different corruption levels.
This is done by defining $$\gamma_t$$ for VDMs and $$\alpha_t$$ for MDMs differently.
In our experiments, we use the following definitions:

| VDM | $$\gamma_t$$ | MDM | $$\alpha_t$$ |
| :-- | :--: | :-- | :--: |
| **Linear log-SNR** | $$\gamma_{\max}+(\gamma_{\min}-\gamma_{\max})t$$ | **Log-linear** | $$1-t$$ |
| **Quadratic log-SNR** | $$\gamma_{\max}+(\gamma_{\min}-\gamma_{\max})t^2$$ | **Cosine** | $$\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)$$ |
| **Cosine** | $$2\log\tan\left(\theta_{\max}+t(\theta_{\min}-\theta_{\max})\right)$$ | **Cosine (DUO)** | $$1-\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)$$ |

where $$\theta_{\min} = \arctan\left(e^{\gamma_{\min}/2}\right)$$ and $$\theta_{\max} = \arctan\left(e^{\gamma_{\max}/2}\right)$$.

### Denoiser Implementation
{: #denoiser-implementation }
We implement the denoiser with `ComposedDenoiser`, combining an input adapter $$A_{\text{in}}(\cdot)$$, a neural network $$\mathrm{NN}_\theta(\cdot)$$, and an output adapter $$A_{\text{out}}(\cdot)$$.
The input adapter transforms the corrupted state $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ into a representation which is usable by the network $$\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{in}}$$.
The output adapter transforms the network's outputs $$\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{feat}}$$ to a format usable by the diffusion formaulation $$\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{out}}$$: 

$$
\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{in}} = A_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{z}_t), \qquad \mathbf{h}_t^{\text{feat}} = \text{NN}_\theta(\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{in}}, \nu_t), \qquad \mathbf{h}_t^{\text{out}} = A_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{feat}}, \mathbf{z}_t).
$$

Here, the output adapter also gets $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ as a reference, if needed, to construct $$\mathbf{h}_t^{\text{out}}$$.

Each vocabulary $$\mathcal{W}$$ contains the clean values plus a `[MASK]` token, giving $$K$$ tokens in total:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{img}} &= \{0, \ldots, 255\} \cup \{\texttt{[MASK]}\}, && K = 257, \\
\mathcal{W}_{\text{text}} &= \{0, \ldots, 50256\} \cup \{\texttt{[MASK]}\}, && K = 50258, \\
\mathcal{W}_{\text{sudoku}} &= \{1, \ldots, 9\} \cup \{\texttt{[MASK]}\}, && K = 10,

For image MDMs, $$A_{\mathrm{in}}(\cdot)$$ maps each token in $$\mathcal{W}_{\text{img}}$$ to evenly spaced values in $$[-1, 1)$$:

$$
\mathcal{G} = \{2j/K - 1 : j = 0, \ldots, K - 1\},
$$

where `[MASK]` ($$j = 256$$) maps to the largest value, so the network effectively sees it as *slightly brighter than white* ($$j = 255$$).
The U-Net also outputs two values per channel $$2C$$, unlike the image VDM U-Net.
These parameterise the location and a log-scale in a discretised logistic distribution over pixel values <d-cite key="austin2021structured"></d-cite>. 
This ensures that neighbouring intensities recieve similar probabilities, not needed in the text/Sudoku output adapters.

### Diffusion Formulation Implementation
{: #diffusion-formulation-implementation }
The diffusion formulation implements family-specific methods that shared algorithms call from the `Diffusion` base class.
The software diagram shows how these are split over algorithms:
- training and likelihoods: `sample_forward_marginal`, `error` and `loss_weight`,
- sampling: `sample_forward_posterior` and `sample_terminal`.

The one method all algorithms need is `predict_clean`.
For each algorithm, we compare the methods it uses across VDMs and MDMs, in maths and in code.

| | VDM | MDM (per position $$i$$) |
| :-- | :--: | :--: |
| **Terminal** $$\mathbf{z}_1 \sim p(\mathbf{z}_1)$$ <br> `sample_terminal` | $$\mathbf{z}_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$ | $$\mathbf{z}_{1,i} = \mathbf{x}_i \; \text{ if } i \in \mathcal{K} \; \text{ else } \; \mathbf{m}$$ |
| **Forward Posterior** <br> $$\mathbf{z}_s \sim q(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x})$$ <br> `sample_forward_posterior` | $$\mathbf{z}_s = \pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}} + \sigma_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}}\pmb{\epsilon}$$ <br> $$\pmb{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$ | $$\mathbf{z}_{s,i} = \mathbf{z}_{t,i} \;\text{ if } \mathbf{z}_{t,i} \neq \mathbf{m}$$ <br> $$\text{ else } \; c_i\,\mathbf{x}_i + (1 - c_i)\,\mathbf{m}$$, <br> $$c_i \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho_{s \mid t})$$ |
| **Forward Marginal** <br> $$\mathbf{z}_t \sim q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x})$$ <br> `sample_forward_marginal` | $$\mathbf{z}_t = \alpha_t \mathbf{x} + \sigma_t \pmb{\epsilon}$$ <br> $$\pmb{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$ | $$\mathbf{z}_{t,i} = b_{t,i}\,\mathbf{x}_i + (1 - b_{t,i})\,\mathbf{m}$$, <br> $$b_{t,i} = 1 \; \mathrm{if} \; i \in \mathcal{K} \; \mathrm{else}$$ <br> $$b_{t,i} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\alpha_t)$$ |
| **Loss Weight** $$\omega(t)$$ <br> `loss_weight` | $$-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SNR}'(t) = -\frac{1}{2}e^{\gamma_t}\gamma_t'$$ | $$-\frac{\alpha_t'}{1-\alpha_t} = \gamma_t'$$ |
| **Error** $$\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}})$$ <br> `error` | $$\frac{1}{N} \lVert \mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert_2^2$$ <br> Mean Squared Error | $$- \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[\mathbf{z}_{t,i} = \mathbf{m}] \log \langle \mathbf{x}_i, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i \rangle$$ <br> Per-Token Cross-Entropy |

#### Predicting Clean Data
Every shared algorithm is written in terms of a clean-data prediction $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}$$, provided by `predict_clean`.
We choose $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}$$ because MDMs naturally predict clean tokens.
Expressing VDMs in terms of $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}$$ as well, rather than the more common $$\hat{\pmb{\epsilon}}$$, makes the parallels between the two families clear, and enables our abstraction for shared algorithms.

The denoiser, however, need not output $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}$$ directly.
A VDM's network could equally predict $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$$, $$\hat{\mathbf{v}}$$ or $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}$$, while an MDM's network outputs logits. 
The `predict_clean` method accounts for these differences, such that shared algorithms need not account for the denoiser's output.

For MDMs, we simply pass through the logits output by the denoiser.

For VDMs, the denoiser predicts the velocity $$\hat{\mathbf{v}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t)$$ <d-cite key="salimans2022progressive"></d-cite>, where $$\mathbf{v} = (\alpha_t \mathbf{z}_t - \mathbf{x}) / \sigma_t$$. 
Here, `predict_clean` converts the denoiser's output to clean data prediction via

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t) = \alpha_t \mathbf{z}_t - \sigma_t \hat{\mathbf{v}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t).
$$

We do this conversion in the diffusion formulation because our interface gives it access to $$\alpha_t$$ and $$\sigma_t$$ through the `ScheduleState`.
This can't be done in the denoiser, as it only sees $$\nu_t$$.

To understand why we predict $$\mathbf{v}$$, we can write the VDM's weighted error (defined in the next section) in terms of the denoiser's output.
Given that $$\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} = -\sigma_t(\mathbf{v} - \hat{\mathbf{v}})$$ and $$e^{\gamma_t}\sigma_t^2 = \alpha_t^2$$,

$$
\underbrace{-\tfrac{1}{2} e^{\gamma_t} \gamma_t'}_{\omega_{\mathbf{x}}(t)}
\lVert \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert^2_2
= \underbrace{-\tfrac{1}{2} \alpha_t^2 \gamma_t'}_{\omega_{\mathbf{v}}(t)}
\lVert \mathbf{v} - \hat{\mathbf{v}} \rVert^2_2,
$$

where $$\omega_{\mathbf{v}}(t)$$ is bounded since $$\alpha_t \leq 1$$.
Had the denoiser output $$\mathbf{x}$$ directly, the loss would be weighted by $$\omega_{\mathbf{x}}(t)$$, which grows exponentially in $$\gamma_t$$ and destabilises gradients at low noise<d-footnote>JiT <d-cite key="li2026back"></d-cite> shows that $$\mathbf{x}$$-prediction is preferred for high-dimensional pixel-space generation. We use $$\mathbf{v}$$-prediction with an $$\mathbf{x}$$-loss for pedagogical purposes, so that VDMs and MDMs can share their $$\mathbf{x}$$-loss algorithms. For our relatively low-dimensional data ($$32 \times 32 \times 3$$), this gave the most stable training and sufficient image quality within our budget when compared against $$\pmb{\epsilon}$$ and $$\mathbf{x}$$-prediction.</d-footnote>.

#### Training & Likelihood-Bound Estimation

Training samples a time $$t$$, corrupts $$\mathbf{x}$$ to $$\mathbf{z}_t$$, and computes the weighted error $$\omega(t) \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}})$$.
Estimating the denoising contribution to the likelihood bound uses the same steps.

| | VDM | MDM (per position $$i$$) |
| :-- | :--: | :--: |
| **Forward Marginal** <br> $$\mathbf{z}_t \sim q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x})$$ <br> `sample_forward_marginal` | $$\mathbf{z}_t = \alpha_t \mathbf{x} + \sigma_t \pmb{\epsilon}$$ <br> $$\pmb{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$ | $$\mathbf{z}_{t,i} = b_{t,i}\,\mathbf{x}_i + (1 - b_{t,i})\,\mathbf{m}$$ <br> $$b_{t,i} = 1 \; \mathrm{if} \; i \in \mathcal{K} \; \mathrm{else}$$ <br> $$b_{t,i} \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\alpha_t)$$ |
| **Loss Weight** $$\omega(t)$$ <br> `loss_weight` | $$-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SNR}'(t) = -\frac{1}{2}e^{\gamma_t}\gamma_t'$$ | $$-\frac{\alpha_t'}{1-\alpha_t} = \gamma_t'$$ |
| **Error** $$\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}})$$ <br> `error` | $$\frac{1}{N} \lVert \mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert_2^2$$ <br> Mean Squared Error | $$- \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[\mathbf{z}_{t,i} = \mathbf{m}] \log \langle \mathbf{x}_i, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i \rangle$$ <br> Per-Token Cross-Entropy |

**Forward Marginal.** The distributions in both cases are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{VDM: } & \quad q(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\alpha_t \mathbf{x},\,
\sigma_t^2 \mathbf{I}) \\
\text{MDM: } & \quad q(\mathbf{z}_{t,i} \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \operatorname{Cat}\left(\alpha_t
\mathbf{x}_i + (1 - \alpha_t)\,\mathbf{m}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Both can be written as sampling rules that transform $$\mathbf{x}$$ with independent noise, as shown in the table.
For VDMs, this is done via the reparameterisation.
For MDMs, the categorical has only two outcomes per position, the clean token with probability $$\alpha_t$$ or the mask otherwise.
As such, it can be sampled with a single Bernoulli variable $$b_{t,i}$$ that selects between them.
Both factorise over positions in the data; VDMs have a diagonal covariance and MDMs mask each position independently.
However, a VDM corrupts every position with Gaussian noise while the MDM replaces position with $$\mathbf{m}$$.
Additionally, for completion tasks with MDMs such as Sudoku, position in a set $$\mathcal{K}$$ (the `keep` argument in the code) are never masked.
For example, this can be used to provide Sudoku clues as context during training.

**Loss Weight and Error.** Both objectives come from the KL term in $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}$$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{VDM: } & \quad D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) \;\Vert\; p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\operatorname{SNR}(s) - \operatorname{SNR}(t)\right] \lVert \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rVert_2^2 \\
\text{MDM: } & \quad D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) \;\Vert\; p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} -\log \langle \mathbf{x}_i, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i \rangle
\end{aligned}
$$

In the continuous time limit, $$T \to \infty$$, the gap between $$s$$ and $$t$$ becomes very small, and the finite differences become derivatives,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{VDM: } & \quad T[\operatorname{SNR}(s) - \operatorname{SNR}(t)] \to -\operatorname{SNR}'(t) \\
\text{MDM: } & \quad T\frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \to \frac{-\alpha_t'}{1 - \alpha_t},
\end{aligned}
$$

which can be written in terms of $$\gamma_t$$ to give the loss weights in the table.
This makes the two implementations of `loss_weight` almost identical, as both differentiate $$\gamma_t$$ with respect to $$t$$, without needing to know which noise schedule produced them.

#### Sampling

Generation starts from a terminal sample $$\mathbf{z}_1$$ at $$t = 1$$ and repeatedly samples the reverse transitions defined by the forward posterior, $$p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t) = q(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x} = \hat{\mathbf{x}})$$.
Here, $$\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \nu_t)$$ is provided by the denoiser via `predict_clean`.

| | VDM | MDM (per position $$i$$) |
| :-- | :--: | :--: |
| **Terminal** $$\mathbf{z}_1 \sim p(\mathbf{z}_1)$$ <br> `sample_terminal` | $$\mathbf{z}_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$ | $$\mathbf{z}_{1,i} = \mathbf{x}_i \; \text{ if } i \in \mathcal{K} \; \text{ else } \; \mathbf{m}$$ |
| **Forward Posterior** <br> $$\mathbf{z}_s \sim q(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x})$$ <br> `sample_forward_posterior` | $$\mathbf{z}_s = \pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}} + \sigma_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}}\pmb{\epsilon}$$ <br> $$\pmb{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$ | $$\mathbf{z}_{s,i} = \mathbf{z}_{t,i} \;\text{ if } \mathbf{z}_{t,i} \neq \mathbf{m},$$ <br> $$\text{ else } \; c_i\,\mathbf{x}_i + (1 - c_i)\,\mathbf{m}$$ <br> $$c_i \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho_{s \mid t})$$ |

**Terminal Distribution.** For VDMs, $$\alpha_1 \approx 0$$ and $$\sigma_1 \approx 1$$, so $$q(\mathbf{z}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) \approx q(\mathbf{z}_1) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$ and generation starts from pure noise.
For MDMs, generation starts from the fully masked state $$\mathbf{m}$$.
We can also start with the positions in $$\mathcal{K}$$ unmasked, turning unconditional generation into completion, such as finishing a sentence or solving Sudoku from its clues.

**Forward Posterior.** For VDMs, the forward posterior is Gaussian,

$$
\begin{gathered}
q(\mathbf{z}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}\left(\pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}}, \sigma^2_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}} \mathbf{I}\right), \\
\pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}} = \frac{\alpha_{t \mid s} \sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2} \mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\alpha_s \sigma_{t \mid s}^2}{\sigma_t^2} \mathbf{x},
\qquad
\sigma^2_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}} = \frac{\sigma_{t \mid s}^2 \sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2},
\end{gathered}
$$

with $$\alpha_{t \mid s} = \alpha_t / \alpha_s$$ and $$\sigma_{t \mid s}^2 = \sigma_t^2 - \alpha_{t \mid s}^2 \sigma_s^2$$.
Here, $$\pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t, \mathbf{x}}$$ merges the current state $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ with clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$.
As before, reparameterisation gives the sampling rule in the table.

For MDMs, the forward posterior acts on each position independently,

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_{s,i} \mid \mathbf{z}_{t,i}, \mathbf{x}_i)
= \begin{cases}
\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{t,i}} & \text{if } \mathbf{z}_{t,i} \neq \mathbf{m} \\
\operatorname{Cat}\left(\rho_{s \mid t}\,\mathbf{x}_i
+ (1 - \rho_{s \mid t})\,\mathbf{m}\right) & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\qquad
\rho_{s \mid t} = \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t},
$$

where $$\rho_{s \mid t}$$ is the probability that a position masked at time $$t$$ is unmasked at time $$s$$.
For unmasked positions, the point mass $$\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{t,i}}$$ shows that they are simply copied to the next timestep unchanged.
For masked positions, the categorical distribution has two outcomes; as before, a single Bernoulli variable $$c_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho_{s \mid t})$$ selects between $$\mathbf{x}_i$$ and $$\mathbf{m}$$.
Together, these give the sampling rule in the table.

    x: torch.Tensor,

    alpha_t, alpha_s = state_t.alpha, state_s.alpha

    x_hat = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=x).sample()

    unmask_mask = torch.bernoulli(unmask_prob).bool()

    visible = z_t != self.mask_token_id

    # MDLM Eq 7 - pass through z_t (z_t ≠ mask) or use z_s_candidates (z_t = mask)
    z_s = torch.where(visible, z_t, z_s_candidates)

From the forward marginal, recall that $$\alpha_t$$ is the retention probability at time $$t$$, so $$1 - \alpha_t$$ is the fraction of positions that are masked.
In the forward posterior, a fraction $$\rho_{s \mid t}$$ of the masked positions are revealed from time $$t$$ to $$s$$.
It follows that each reverse step reveals an additional fraction $$(1 - \alpha_t)\,\rho_{s \mid t} = \alpha_s - \alpha_t$$.
Excluding positions in $$\mathcal{K}$$, which are visible from $$t = 1$$, the expected number of tokens revealed by time $$s$$ is a telescoping sum over the sampling steps,

$$
\sum_{\text{steps}} (N - |\mathcal{K}|)(\alpha_s - \alpha_t) = (N - |\mathcal{K}|)(\alpha_s - \alpha_1) \approx (N - |\mathcal{K}|)\alpha_s,
$$

since $$\alpha_1 \approx 0$$. For unconditional generation ($$\mathcal{K} = \emptyset$$), this becomes $$N\alpha_s$$.

**Remasking Forward Posterior.**
Once an MDM reveals a position, it can never be changed. 
ReMDM <d-cite key="wang2025remasking"></d-cite> instead remasks previously unmasked positions with probability $$\xi_t$$:

$$
q_\xi(\mathbf{z}_{s,i} \mid \mathbf{z}_{t,i}, \mathbf{x}_i)
= \begin{cases}
\operatorname{Cat}\left((1 - \xi_t) \mathbf{x}_i + \xi_{t, i} \mathbf{m}\right)
& \text{if } \mathbf{z}_{t,i} \neq \mathbf{m} \\
\operatorname{Cat}\left(\frac{\alpha_s - (1 - \xi_{t,i}) \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}\,
\mathbf{x}_i + \frac{1 - \alpha_s - \xi_{t,i} \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t}\,
\mathbf{m}\right) & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$

where $$0 \leq \xi_t \leq \min\{1, (1 - \alpha_s)/\alpha_t\}$$.
Setting $$\xi_t = 0$$ recovers the MDM forward posterior.
Additionally, the forward marginal remains unchanged.
As such, the resulting objective is a reweighting of the MDM objective, so the remasking posterior can be used to sample a pretrained MDM.

How $$\xi_t$$ is allocated across positions is a design choice. We compare two strategies:

1. **Uniform remasking (ReMDM-unif)** uses the same probability $$\xi_t$$ for every visible position.
2. **Confidence-based remasking (ReMDM-conf)** assigns a per-position probability $$\xi_{t,i}$$, favouring positions whose tokens were decoded with low confidence.

We implement ReMDM as a subclass of `MDM` that overrides two methods: `sample_forward_posterior` implements the remasking posterior above and `sample_reverse_transition` computes the confidence of unmasking  since that requires the model's prediction
$$\hat{\mathbf{x}}$$. ReMDM-conf then uses these confidences to set
$$\xi_{t,i}$$.

#### Loss: Prediction Error and Loss Weight

The prediction error $$\mathcal{E}$$ and its corresponding loss weight $$\omega(t)$$ differ between state-spaces:

|  | Gaussian VP | Masked Categorical |
| :-- | :--: | :--: |
| **Prediction error** $$\mathcal{E}$$ | $$\mid\mid \mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}} \mid\mid_2^2$$ | $$- \sum_{i} \mathbf{1} [\mathbf{z}_{t,i} = \mathbf{m}] \log \langle \mathbf{x}_i, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i \rangle$$ |
| **Loss weight** $$\omega(t)$$ | $$-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SNR}'(t)$$ | $$-\frac{\alpha_t'}{1-\alpha_t}$$ |
| **Loss weight using $$\gamma_t$$** | $$-\frac{1}{2}e^{\gamma_t}\gamma_t'$$ | $$\gamma_t'$$ |
| **Noise level** $$\nu_t$$ | $$\log\operatorname{SNR}(t)$$ | $$-\log\alpha_t$$ |
| **Interpretation** | Weighted squared error | Weighted categorical cross-entropy |

The $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}^T$$ term of the variational objective contains the KL divergence $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\;\Vert\;p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t)\right)$$, whose expansion depends on the state space.
For both models, this term reduces to a finite difference coefficient multiplied by a prediction error $$\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}})$$.
In the limit $$T \rightarrow \infty$$, the finite difference term turns into the loss weight $$\omega(t)$$.

For Gaussian diffusion, expanding the KL divergence gives

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) \;\Vert\; p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t)\right)
=
\frac{1}{2}\left[ \operatorname{SNR}(s) - \operatorname{SNR}(t) \right] \left\| \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \right\|_2^2,
$$

where $$\operatorname{SNR}(t) = \frac{\alpha_t^2}{\sigma_t^2}$$.
Using the diffusion times as $$s=t-\frac{1}{T}$$, the finite difference satisfies

$$
T\left[\operatorname{SNR}(s)-\operatorname{SNR}(t)\right] \xrightarrow{T\to\infty} -\operatorname{SNR}'(t)
$$

The continuous-time Gaussian objective therefore uses

$$
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{VDM}}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_2^2,
\qquad
\omega_{\mathrm{VDM}}(t) = -\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{SNR}'(t).
$$

Using the corruption coordinate $$\gamma_t=\log\operatorname{SNR}(t)$$, this weight can be written as

$$
\omega_{\mathrm{VDM}}(t) = -\frac{1}{2} e^{\gamma_t} \gamma_t'.
$$

For masked categorical diffusion, expanding the same KL divergence via the SUBS parameterisation gives the Rao-Blackwellised objective

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) \;\Vert\; p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t)\right)

\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t} \, - \log \langle \mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rangle.

Again, using $$s = t - \frac{1}{T}$$, the weighting becomes

$$
T \left(\alpha_s - \alpha_t\right) \xrightarrow{T\to\infty} -\alpha_t'

The continuous-time masked categorical objective therefore uses

$$
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{MDLM}}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}) = -\log \langle \mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}} \rangle,
\qquad
\omega_{\mathrm{MDLM}}(t) = -\frac{\alpha_t'}{1-\alpha_t}.
$$

Due to the zero-masking probabilities and carry-over unmasking in MDLMs, unmamsked tokens contribute zero to the loss, hence 

$$
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{MDLM}}(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{x}}) = - \sum_{i} \mathbf{1} [\mathbf{z}_{t,i} = \mathbf{m}] \log \langle \mathbf{x}_i, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i \rangle .
$$

Using the corruption coordinates $$\gamma_t = \log(1-\alpha_t)$$, we have $$\gamma_t' = -\frac{\alpha_t'}{1-\alpha_t}$$, hence

$$
\omega_{\mathrm{MDLM}}(t) = \gamma_t'.
$$

#### Forward Posterior

Given a corrupted state $$\mathbf{z}_t$$, clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$, and the `ScheduleState`s at times $$s<t$$, `sample_forward_posterior` samples an earlier, less-corrupted state $$\mathbf{z}_s$$ from $$q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})$$.

|  | Gaussian VP | Masked Categorical |
| :-- | :--: | :--: |
| **Forward posterior** | $$\mathcal{N}\left(\pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t,x}(\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x}),\sigma_{s \mid t,x}^2\mathbf{I}\right)$$ | $$\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{t,i}}$$ if $$\mathbf{z}_{t,i}\neq\mathbf{m}$$; otherwise $$\operatorname{Cat}\!\left(\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_i+\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\mathbf{m}\right)$$ |
| **Sampling rule** | $$\mathbf{z}_s = \pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t,x}(\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x}) + \sigma_{s \mid t,x} \pmb{\epsilon}$$ <br> where $$\pmb{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$ | Visible coordinates are carried over; masked coordinates either reveal the clean value or remain masked |
| **Interpretation** | Combines the current corrupted state with the clean data | Progressively reveals clean values |

In both cases, the factors of the forward process form conjugate pairs for the Bayesian update, allowing the forward posterior to be evaluated in closed form.

For Gaussian diffusion, the posterior remains Gaussian. Its mean combines the observed corrupted state $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ with the clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$, while its variance captures the remaining uncertainty about the earlier state $$\mathbf{z}_s$$,

$$
\pmb{\mu}_{s \mid t,x}(\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{x})
=
\frac{\alpha_{t \mid s} \sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2} \mathbf{z}_t
+
\frac{\alpha_s \sigma_{t \mid s}^2}{\sigma_t^2} \mathbf{x},

\qquad

\sigma_{s \mid t,x}^2
=
\frac{\sigma_{t\mid s}^2\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}.
$$

where $$\alpha_{t \mid s} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$$ and $$\sigma^2_{t \mid s} = \sigma^2_{t} - \alpha^2_{t \mid s} \sigma^2_{s}$$.

For masked categorical diffusion, the posterior is determined coordinate-wise by whether the clean value has already been revealed.
Here, $$\mathbf{m}$$ denotes the mask state, while $$\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{t,i}}$$ denotes a point mass at the already-visible value $$\mathbf{z}_{t,i}$$. 
A visible coordinate is therefore carried over. 
If a coordinate is masked at time $$t$$, it remains masked at the earlier time $$s$$ with probability

$$
q(z_{s,i}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,i}=\mathbf{m},x_i)
=
\frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t},
$$

and reveals its clean value with probability

$$
q(z_{s,i}=x_i\mid z_{t,i}=\mathbf{m},x_i)
=
\frac{\alpha_s-\alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t}.
$$

These terms are combined in the masked categorical forward posterior.

In Gaussian diffusion, uncertainty about the cleaner state at $$s$$ is continuous via the posterior mean and variance.
In masked categorical diffusion, this uncertainty is discrete; the clean value is either selected by time $$t$$ or there is uncertainty over unmasking this value for time $$s$$.

When the forward posterior is used to construct the reverse transition, the unknown clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$ is replaced by the neural network prediction. For Gaussian diffusion this is a predicted clean value, while for masked categorical diffusion it is a distribution over the $$K$$ clean categories; the forward posterior then introduces the additional mask state when sampling $$\mathbf{z}_s$$.


#### Terminal Distribution

The terminal distribution $$p(\mathbf{z}_1) = q(\mathbf{z}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) \approx q(\mathbf{z}_1)$$ provides the maximally corrupted, data-independent state from which generation begins.

|  | Gaussian VP | Masked Categorical |
| :-- | :--: | :--: |
| **Terminal forward marginal** | $$q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_1\mathbf{x},\sigma_1^2\mathbf{I})$$ | $$q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})=\delta_{\mathbf{m}}$$ when $$\alpha_1=0$$ |
| **Terminal distribution** $$p(\mathbf{z}_1)$$ | $$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$ | $$\delta_{\mathbf{m}}$$ |
| **Sampling rule** | $$\mathbf{z}_1\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$ | $$\mathbf{z}_1=\mathbf{m}$$ |
| **Interpretation** | Random Gaussian noise | Fully masked state |

For variance-preserving Gaussian diffusion, the schedule is chosen so that as $$t \rightarrow 1$$ the signal coefficient approaches zero $$\alpha_t \rightarrow 0$$ while the noise coefficient approaches one as $$\sigma_1 \rightarrow 1$$.
Hence, the terminal forward marginal becomes independent of the clean data,

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),
$$

which motivates choosing the standard Gaussian as the terminal distribution $$p(\mathbf{x})$$.

## Closing Thoughts
The difference in continuous and discrete diffusion models can obscure their shared underlying structure, particularly when codebases do not map easily to the equations in papers.

In this post, we reveal their similarities by decomposing VDMs and MDMs into three components: a noise schedule (`NoiseSchedule`), a denoiser (`ComposedDenoiser`) and a diffusion formulation (`Diffusion`).
This general software abstraction allows us to change schedules, architectures and modelling assumptions while reusing the same training, sampling and likelihood-bound estimation code. This allows us to easily move between image generation, text generation and Sudoku solving.

In particular, by separating the noise schedule from the diffusion formulation, we make its role explicit — providing the corruption level associated with each timestep during training, sampling and likelihood-bound estimation.
By making the denoiser independent of the diffusion formulation, we show how the same architectures can be used under different modelling assumptions.
Finally, by separating the noise schedule from the denoiser, we made clear that noise levels describe the corruption process independently of the neural network.

**Using our software interface we also highliught some interesting results...**
- Unmasking tracks expected unmasking
- understanding remasking

We encourage readers to extend our abstraction in their own work on generative modelling.
Our setup and design patterns naturally lend themselves to flow-matching <d-cite key="gao2025diffusionmeetsflow"></d-cite><d-cite key="hu2026elf"></d-cite>, block diffusion <d-cite key="arriola2025block"></d-cite>, alternative remasking strategies <d-cite key="wang2025remasking"></d-cite><d-cite key="kim2025fine"></d-cite>, latent diffusion <d-cite key="rasal2026steering"></d-cite> and reasoning tasks <d-cite key="svete2026on"></d-cite><d-cite key="horvitz2026rethinking"></d-cite>.
We hope this approach makes popular diffusion models easier to understand and lowers the barrier to entry for readers exploring this field.

## Appendix

### Notation and conventions

- **Data and latents.** $$\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$$ is the clean data and $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ is its corrupted version at time $$t\in[0,1]$$. Time runs from clean to noisy where $$\mathbf{z}_0$$ is the least noisy latent and $$\mathbf{z}_1$$ is the noisiest.
- **Ordering.** Whenever two times appear together, $$s<t$$, so $$\mathbf{z}_s$$ is less noisy than $$\mathbf{z}_t$$
- **Discretisation.** For the lower bound we split $$[0,1]$$ into $$T$$ segments with $$t(i)=i/T$$ and $$s(i)=(i-1)/T$$ for $$i=1,\dots,T$$.
<!-- - **Noise schedule.** $$\alpha_t$$ and $$\sigma_t$$ define the forward marginal, and $$\mathrm{SNR}(t)=\alpha_t^2/\sigma_t^2$$ is decreasing in $$t$$. -->
- **Subscripts.** A plain subscript $$t$$ refers to the forward marginal at time $$t$$ (for example $$\pmb{\mu}_t$$ and $$\pmb{\Sigma}_t$$), a subscript $$t\mid s$$ refers to the forward transition from $$s$$ to $$t$$ (for example $$\alpha_{t\mid s}$$ and $$\sigma^2_{t\mid s}$$), and a subscript $$s\mid t,x$$ refers to the forward posterior of $$\mathbf{z}_s$$ given $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ and $$\mathbf{x}$$ (for example $$\pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x}$$ and $$\sigma^2_{s\mid t,x}$$).
- **Noise variables.** $$\pmb{\epsilon}$$, with any subscript, denotes a standard normal sample $$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$. Noise variables with different subscripts are independent.

### Forward marginal $$q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})$$
{: #forward-marginal-derivation }

The first step is to define how data is corrupted and this is done by prescribing forward marginal $$q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})$$ for $$t\in[0,1]$$. This is the distribution of the corrupted state $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ given the clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$ at time $$t$$ where $$t=0$$ corresponds to (almost) clean data and $$t=1$$ corresponds to the maximally corrupted state. In both cases, the forward marginal is a continuous-time Markov process, which means that the state at time $$t$$ depends only on the state at an earlier time $$s<t$$ and not on any other previous states. In either case, we need to design a forward marginal which sufficiently corrupts the data. 

#### VDM

In the case of VDM, the data is modeled as a continuous variable in $$\mathbb{R}^n$$. The forward marginal is a Gaussian distribution given by

$$q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_t; \alpha_t\mathbf{x}, \sigma^2_t\mathbf{I})$$

where $$\alpha_t$$ and $$\sigma_t$$ are the signal and noise coefficients. $$\alpha_t$$ and $$\sigma_t$$ go from 1 to 0 and 0 to 1, respectively, which are functions of time $$t$$. This ensures that at time $$t=0$$, the corrupted state $$\mathbf{z}_0$$ is equal to the clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$, while at time $$t=1$$, the corrupted state $$\mathbf{z}_1$$ is independent of the clean data and is simply Gaussian noise.

#### MDM

In the case of MDM, the data, $$\mathbf{x} \in \{1, \dots, K\}^D$$, is modeled as a discrete variable. The noising process corresponds to masking coordinates of the the original data $$\mathbf{x}$$. The noising process is MDMs is a continuous-time markov process where at time $$t=0$$, the data is clean, and at time $$t=1$$ all coordinates are masked. This happens independently for each coordinate. Because of this independence, the forward marginal can be factorised as a product of categorical distributions over each coordinate, given by

$$\begin{aligned}
q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})
&= \prod_{d=1}^D q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d)\\
&= \prod_{d=1}^D \alpha_t \delta_{x_d}(z_{t,d}) + (1-\alpha_t) \delta_{m}(z_{t,d})\\
\end{aligned}$$

Where $$\delta_{x_d}$$ is a Dirac delta function centered at the clean value $$x_d$$, and $$\delta_{m}$$ is a Dirac delta function centered at the mask state. This can be equivalently expressed as a product of categorical distributions over each coordinate, which is what is commonly used in the literature <d-cite key="mdm"></d-cite>

$$\begin{aligned}
q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x}) &= \prod_{d=1}^D \operatorname{Cat}\left(z_{t,d}; \alpha_t \hat{\mathbf{x}}_d + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{m}\right)
\end{aligned}$$

Where $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_d$$ is a one-hot vector corresponding to the clean value $$x_d$$, and $$\mathbf{m}$$ is a one-hot vector corresponding to the mask state, which we take as the $$K+1$$-th state. For example, if $$x_{d} = 4$$ then $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{d}$$ would be a vector with a 1 in the 4th entry and 0 elsewhere. 

Importantly, the combination $$\alpha_t \hat{\mathbf{x}}_d + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{m} \in [0,1]^K$$ represents a valid probability distribution over the K categories. For example, if $$K=4$$, $$\alpha_t = 0.7$$, and $$x_d = 2$$ then the probability distribution over the categories would be

$$\alpha_t \hat{\mathbf{x}}_d + (1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{m} = 0.7 * [0, 1, 0, 0] + 0.3 * [0, 0, 0, 1] = [0, 0.7, 0, 0.3]$$

ie, at time t, there is a 70% chance the $$d$$th coordinate is the clean value 2, and a 30% chance it is masked.

All together, we see that the probability that the state is $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ given the clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$ is a product of each of the coordinates being either the clean value or the mask state, with probabilities determined by $$\alpha_t$$.

### Forward transition $$q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s)$$
{: #forward-transition-derivation }

From the defined forward marginals, we need to derive transition steps, which give the probability of transitioning from $$\mathbf{z}_s$$ at time $$s$$ to $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ at time $$t$$. Next we can find the forward transition $$q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s)$$ for $$0 \leq s < t \leq 1$$ as it will be useful later. 

#### VDM

In the case of VDM, the forward marginals are Gaussian, and so the forward transitions, $$q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s)$$,are assumed to be gaussian, following

$$ \mathbf{z}_t \stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}, \qquad \pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$$

To find the parameters, $$\alpha_{t\mid s}$$ and $$\sigma_{t\mid s}$$, of the forward transition, we can use moment matching. As we know that 

$$\mathbf{z}_t \stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_t\mathbf{x} + \sigma_t\pmb{\epsilon}_t \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{z}_s \stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_s\mathbf{x} + \sigma_s\pmb{\epsilon}_s$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{z}_t 
&\stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\\
&\stackrel{d}{=} \alpha_{t\mid s}(\alpha_s\mathbf{x} + \sigma_s\pmb{\epsilon}_s) + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}
\end{aligned}
$$

and so because the forward marginals are Gaussian, we can match the first and second moments to find the parameters, $$\alpha_{t\mid s}$$ and $$\sigma_{t\mid s}$$. As the first moments are equal, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_t\mid \mathbf{x}] &= \mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\mid \mathbf{x}] \\
&= \alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{x}] + \sigma_{t\mid s}\mathbb{E}[\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\mid \mathbf{x}] \\
&= \alpha_{t\mid s}(\alpha_s\mathbf{x}) + \sigma_{t\mid s}(\mathbf{0}) \\
&= \alpha_{t\mid s}\alpha_s\mathbf{x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $$\alpha_{t\mid s} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$$.

As the second moments are equal, and $$\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}$$ is independent of $$\mathbf{z}_s$$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}[\mathbf{z}_t\mid \mathbf{x}] &= \operatorname{Var}[\alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\mid \mathbf{x}] \\
&= \alpha_{t\mid s}^2\operatorname{Var}[\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{x}] + \sigma_{t\mid s}^2\operatorname{Var}[\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}\mid \mathbf{x}] \\
&= \alpha_{t\mid s}^2(\sigma_s^2\mathbf{I}) + \sigma_{t\mid s}^2(\mathbf{I}) \\
&= (\alpha_{t\mid s}^2\sigma_s^2 + \sigma_{t\mid s}^2)\mathbf{I}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $$\sigma_{t\mid s}^2 = \sigma_t^2 - \alpha_{t\mid s}^2\sigma_s^2 = \sigma_t^2 - \frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^2$$

#### MDM

In the case of MDMs, we once again use the coordinate-wise independence to decompose the forward transition into a product of coordinate-wise transitions,

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s) = \prod_{d=1}^D q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d})
$$

Using this, we can derive the forward transition for each coordinate, $$q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d})$$. As we know the marginal is given by $$q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d)$$, we need a forward markov transition (ie. only depends on current state) that is consistent with the marginal. This can be derived using the chapman–kolmogorov equation or law of total probability, which gives

$$\begin{aligned}
q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d) 
&= \sum_{z_{s,d}} q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d}, x_d)\,q(z_{s,d}\mid x_d)\\
&= \sum_{z_{s,d}} q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d})\,q(z_{s,d}\mid x_d)
\end{aligned}$$

This equation is pretty much saying that the probability of a coordinate being in a certain state at time $$t$$, given the input, is the sum of the probabilities of all possible transitions from time $$s$$ to time $$t$$, weighted by the probability of the intermediate state. However, as the forward transition is markov, we can drop the dependency on $$x_d$$ in the first term. Additionally, becase we know that the only possible values for the forward marginal given $$x_d$$ are $$x_d$$ and the mask state $$\mathbf{m}$$, we can simplify the sum to only include these two states, giving

$$\begin{aligned}
q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d) = q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d) + q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d}=m)\,q(z_{s,d}=m\mid x_d)
\end{aligned}$$

When considering the case where $$z_{t,d} = x_d$$, we have, 

$$\begin{aligned}
q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid x_d)
&= \alpha_t \\
&= q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d) + q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=m)\,q(z_{s,d}=m\mid x_d)\\
&= q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,\alpha_s + q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=m)\,(1-\alpha_s)\\
&= q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,\alpha_s + 0\cdot(1-\alpha_s)\\
&= q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,\alpha_s
\end{aligned}$$

where we use the defined marginals to get $$q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d) = \alpha_s, q(z_{s,d}=m\mid x_d) = 1-\alpha_s$$ and $$q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid x_d) = \alpha_{t}$$ and also the fact that if $$z_{s,d}$$ is masked, then $$z_{t,d}$$ cannot be the clean value $$x_d$$, so $$q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=m) = 0$$.

Denoting $$q(z_{t,d}=x_d\mid z_{s,d}=x_d) = \alpha_{t\mid s}$$, we have

$$\alpha_t = \alpha_{t\mid s}\,\alpha_s \implies \alpha_{t\mid s} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$$

So the probability that a coordinate is the clean value at time $$t$$ given that it was the clean value at time $$s$$ is the ratio of the probabilities of being the clean value at time $$t$$ and time $$s$$.


### Forward posterior $$q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})$$
{: #forward-posterior-derivation }

Another useful quantity is the forward posterior, $$q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})$$ for $$0 \leq s < t \leq 1$$, which can be derived using the conditional Bayes' rule

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \frac{q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{z}_s)\,q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}
$$

#### VDM

In the case of VDM, the forward marginals and forward transitions are Gaussian, so $$\mathbf{z}_s$$ and $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ are jointly Gaussian given $$\mathbf{x}$$, and hence the forward posterior is also Gaussian,

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_s;\ \pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x},\ \pmb{\Sigma}_{s\mid t,x})
$$

The mean and covariance follow from the standard identities for conditioning a multivariate Gaussian [cite this],

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x} &= \pmb{\mu}_s + \pmb{\Sigma}_{s,t}\pmb{\Sigma}_t^{-1}(\mathbf{z}_t - \pmb{\mu}_t) \\
\pmb{\Sigma}_{s\mid t,x} &= \pmb{\Sigma}_s - \pmb{\Sigma}_{s,t}\pmb{\Sigma}_t^{-1}\pmb{\Sigma}_{t,s}
\end{aligned}
$$

where, given $$\mathbf{x}$$, the moments of the forward marginals are

$$
\pmb{\mu}_s = \alpha_s\mathbf{x}, \qquad \pmb{\mu}_t = \alpha_t\mathbf{x}, \qquad \pmb{\Sigma}_s = \sigma_s^2\mathbf{I}, \qquad \pmb{\Sigma}_t = \sigma_t^2\mathbf{I}
$$

For the cross-covariance, write $$\mathbf{z}_t = \alpha_{t\mid s}\mathbf{z}_s + \sigma_{t\mid s}\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}$$ with $$\pmb{\epsilon}_{t\mid s}$$ independent of $$\mathbf{z}_s$$. Then

$$
\pmb{\Sigma}_{s,t} = \operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{z}_s,\mathbf{z}_t] = \alpha_{t\mid s}\operatorname{Var}[\mathbf{z}_s] = \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\sigma_s^2\mathbf{I}
$$

Substituting these in, we get the mean and covariance of the forward posterior,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x}
&= \alpha_s\mathbf{x} + \frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}\sigma_s^2\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_t^2}\left(\mathbf{z}_t - \alpha_t\mathbf{x}\right) \\[6pt]
&= \frac{\alpha_t\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \alpha_s\left(1 - \frac{\alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2}\right)\mathbf{x} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{\alpha_t\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2 - \alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{x}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pmb{\Sigma}_{s\mid t,x}
&= \sigma_s^2\mathbf{I} - \frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^4\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_t^2}\mathbf{I} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}\left(\sigma_t^2 - \frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^2\right)\mathbf{I} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{\sigma_s^2\left(\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2 - \alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2\right)}{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{I}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_s;\ \frac{\alpha_t\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2 - \alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2}{\alpha_s\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{x},\ \frac{\sigma_s^2\left(\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2 - \alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2\right)}{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{I}\right)
$$

Using $$\alpha_{t\mid s}=\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}$$ and $$\sigma^2_{t\mid s}=\sigma_t^2-\frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^2$$ from the transition, this can be written more compactly as

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_s;\ \frac{\alpha_{t\mid s}\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\alpha_s\sigma^2_{t\mid s}}{\sigma_t^2}\,\mathbf{x},\ \frac{\sigma^2_{t\mid s}\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}\mathbf{I}\right)
$$

#### MDM

For the case of MDM, we use the coordinate wise independece to factorise the forward posterior, which is given by

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{d=1}^D q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)
$$

Then we can apply the conditional Bayes' rule to each coordinate, giving

$$
q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d) = \frac{q(z_{t,d}\mid z_{s,d})\,q(z_{s,d}\mid x_d)}{q(z_{t,d}\mid x_d)}
$$

In words, this probability gives the probability that a coordinate is in a certain state at an earlier time $$s$$, given that it is in a certain state at a later time $$t$$ and the clean data.

Then to figure out what the forward posterior is, we can consider the possible values of $$z_{t,d}$$ because $$z_{t,d}$$ can only be either the clean value $$x_d$$ ($$x_d$$ cannot be masked by assumption) or the mask state $$\mathbf{m}$$. The cases are as follows:




- Unmaksed later, $$z_{t,d} = x_d$$ and masked earlier, $$z_{s,d} = \mathbf{m}$$
- Unmaksed later, $$z_{t,d} = x_d$$ and unmasked earlier, $$z_{s,d} = x_d$$
- Masked later, $$z_{t,d} = \mathbf{m}$$ and unmasked earlier, $$z_{s,d} = x_d$$
- Masked later, $$z_{t,d} = \mathbf{m}$$ and masked earlier, $$z_{s,d} = \mathbf{m}$$

When $$z_{t,d} = x_d$$, it means that the coordinate is unmasked at time $$t$$, and so it must have been unmasked at time $$s$$. Therefore, we have

- $$q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=x_d, x_d) = 1$$
- $$q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=x_d, x_d) = 0$$

When the coordinate is masked at time $$t$$, it could have been either masked or unmasked at time $$s$$. Therefore, we have

- $$q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{s,d}=x_d) = 1-\alpha_{t\mid s}$$, which is the probability that a coordinate is masked at time $$t$$ given that it was the clean value at time $$s$$.
- $$q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}) = 1$$, which is the probability that a coordinate is masked at time $$t$$ given that it was masked at time $$s$$.
- $$q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid x_d) = 1-\alpha_t$$, which is the probability that a coordinate is masked at time $$t$$ given the clean data.
- $$q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d) = \alpha_s$$, which is the probability that a coordinate is the clean value at time $$s$$ given the clean data.

Then we can use the conditional Bayes' rule to get

$$\begin{aligned}
&q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\\
&= \frac{q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{s,d}=x_d)\,q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid x_d)}{q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid x_d)}\\ 
&= \frac{(1-\alpha_{t\mid s})\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\\
&= \frac{(1-\alpha_t/\alpha_s)\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t}\\
&= \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1-\alpha_t} := \rho_{s\mid t}
\end{aligned}$$

and 

$$\begin{aligned}
&q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\\
&= \frac{q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m})\,q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid x_d)}{q(z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid x_d)}\\
&= \frac{1\cdot(1-\alpha_s)}{1-\alpha_t}\\
&= \frac{1-\alpha_s}{1-\alpha_t} = 1 - \rho_{s\mid t}
\end{aligned}$$

We have now considered all the possible cases for $$z_{t,d}$$ and $$z_{s,d}$$, and so we have derived the forward posterior for MDMs. In summary, we have

$$\begin{aligned}
q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d) =\begin{cases}
\delta_{x_d}(z_{s,d}) & \text{if } z_{t,d} = x_d\\
\rho_{s\mid t}\,\delta_{x_d}(z_{s,d}) + (1-\rho_{s\mid t})\,\delta_{\mathbf{m}}(z_{s,d}) & \text{if } z_{t,d} = \mathbf{m} 
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}$$

Then the forward posterior for the whole state is given by

$$
q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{d=1}^D q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)
$$


### Variational Lower Bound

Following VDM, time is continuous on $$[0,1]$$, with $$\mathbf{z}_0$$ the least noisy latent and $$\mathbf{z}_1$$ the noisiest. We discretise into $$T$$ segments with $$t(i)=i/T$$ and $$s(i)=(i-1)/T$$ for $$i=1,\dots,T$$, so each step goes from $$\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}$$ to $$\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}$$. We write $$\mathbf{z}_{0:1}$$ for all $$T+1$$ latents.

As we want to maximize the log-likelihood of the data, we can use the variational lower bound to derive a loss function. We can write the log-likelihood as

$$\begin{aligned}
\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})
&= \log \int p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{0:1})\,d\mathbf{z}_{0:1} \\
&= \log \int \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{0:1})}{q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})} q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{z}_{0:1}
\end{aligned}$$

Using the following factorisation of the [xWHAT TO CALL THIS?] and []

$$\begin{aligned}
p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{0:1}) &= p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(1)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(1)})\cdots p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(T)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(T)})p(\mathbf{z}_1)= p(\mathbf{z}_1)\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\prod_{i=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\\
q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x}) &= q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})q(\mathbf{z}_{s(T)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(T)},\mathbf{x})\cdots q(\mathbf{z}_{s(1)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(1)},\mathbf{x}) = q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^T q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})
\end{aligned}$$

we get, using the compact product form, that

$$\begin{aligned}
\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})
&= \log \int \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\prod_{i=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^T q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})} q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\\
&= \log \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\prod_{i=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^T q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})}\right] \\
&\geq \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\prod_{i=1}^T p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})\prod_{i=1}^T q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})}\right] =: 
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})
\end{aligned}$$

where the inequality follows from Jensen's inequality ($$\log \mathbb{E}[f(X)] \geq \mathbb{E}[\log f(X)]$$). We can further simplify the pointwise variational lower bound, $$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})$$, as follows

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}) 
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{0:1}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0) + \sum_{i=1}^{T} \log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})} + \log \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_0\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\right] + \sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)},\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x})}\right] + \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_1)}{q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x})}\right]\\
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_0\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}_0)\right] - \sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\right)\right] - D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p(\mathbf{z}_1)\right)
\end{aligned}$$

In the second equality, we see that the terms in the sum are only dependent on $$z_{s(i)},z_{t(i)}$$ given $$x$$, so the expectation over all $$z_{0:1}$$ is simplified to an expectation over $$z_{s(i)},z_{t(i)}$$ given $$x$$. In the third equality, we split the expectation $$\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{s(i)},z_{t(i)}\mid x)} = \mathbb{E}_{q(z_{t(i)}\mid x)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{s(i)}\mid z_{t(i)},x)}\right]$$ and use the following definiton of the KL divergence

$$
D_{KL}(P\,\|\,Q) = \int P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} dx
$$

#### VDM

To resolve the KL terms, we need to choose the model $$p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)$$. The forward posterior $$q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})$$ is the Gaussian derived above, and it depends on the clean data $$\mathbf{x}$$, which the model doesn't have at sampling time. So we use the same Gaussian with $$\mathbf{x}$$ replaced by a prediction $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)$$ from a neural network

$$
p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t) = q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)) = \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_s;\ \pmb{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t),\ \sigma^2_{s\mid t,x}\mathbf{I}\right)
$$

Both Gaussians in the KL then have the same covariance, $$\sigma^2_{s\mid t,x}\mathbf{I}$$, and differ only in their means. For two Gaussians with equal covariance, the trace term equals $$n$$ and the log-determinant term is zero, so the KL reduces to

$$
D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2_{s\mid t,x}}\left\|\pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x} - \pmb{\mu}_\theta\right\|^2
$$

The means differ only in the coefficient on $$\mathbf{x}$$ (the $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ terms cancel)

$$
\pmb{\mu}_{s\mid t,x} - \pmb{\mu}_\theta = \frac{\alpha_s\sigma^2_{t\mid s}}{\sigma_t^2}\left(\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta\right), \qquad \sigma^2_{s\mid t,x} = \frac{\sigma^2_{t\mid s}\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_t^2}
$$

Substituting,

$$
D_{KL} = \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2\sigma^2_{t\mid s}\sigma_s^2}\cdot\frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma^4_{t\mid s}}{\sigma_t^4}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta\right\|^2 = \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma^2_{t\mid s}}{2\sigma_s^2\sigma_t^2}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta\right\|^2
$$

With $$\mathrm{SNR}(t)=\alpha_t^2/\sigma_t^2$$ and $$\sigma^2_{t\mid s}=\sigma_t^2-\frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}\sigma_s^2$$, we have

$$
\mathrm{SNR}(s)-\mathrm{SNR}(t) = \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma_t^2-\alpha_t^2\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2\sigma_t^2} = \frac{\alpha_s^2\sigma^2_{t\mid s}}{\sigma_s^2\sigma_t^2}
$$

so each diffusion loss term is a weighted MSE

$$
D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{SNR}(s)-\mathrm{SNR}(t)\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)\right\|^2
$$

#### MDM

As done in the VDM case, the model for MDM is given by the forward posterior with $$\mathbf{x}$$ replaced by a prediction $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)$$ from a neural network which outputs a distribution over the possible values for each coordinate of $$\mathbf{x}$$. The forward posterior factorises over coordinates, so we can also factorise the model

$$\begin{aligned}
p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t) 
&= \prod_{d=1}^D p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})
\end{aligned}$$

where 

$$\begin{aligned}
p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})=
\begin{cases}
\delta_{z_{t,d}}(z_{s,d}) & z_{t,d}\neq\mathbf m\\[4pt]
1 - \rho_{s\mid t}\,\delta_{\mathbf m}(z_{s,d})+\,\rho_{s\mid t}\,\hat x_{\theta,d}(z_{s,d};\mathbf z_t,t) & z_{t,d}=\mathbf m
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}$$

where $$\hat{x}_{\theta,d}(z_{s,d};\mathbf{z}_t, t)$$ is the probability that the $$d$$th coordinate of $$\mathbf{x}$$ is $$z_{s,d}$$ given the latent $$\mathbf{z}_t$$ and time $$t$$, as predicted by the neural network.

We first consider a KL term for a timestep which is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
&D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right) \\
&= \sum_{d=1}^D D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\right)
\end{aligned}$$

Next, we consider the term in the sum for a single coordinate,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\right)
\end{aligned}$$

Using the definition of KL, $$D_{KL}(P\,\|\,Q) = \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$$, we can write this as

$$\begin{aligned}
&D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\right)\\
&= \sum_{z_{s, d} \in \{x_d, \mathbf{m}\}} q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\log\frac{q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)}{p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})}
\end{aligned}$$

If $$z_{t,d} = x_d$$ is unmasked, the KL term is zero because the two distributions, $$q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)$$ and $$p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})$$, are identical as $$z_{s,d}$$ is always going to be $$x_d$$. However, if $$z_{t,d} = \mathbf{m}$$ is masked, then $$z_{s,d}$$ can be either $$x_d$$ or $$\mathbf{m}$$, and the KL term is non-zero.

$$\begin{aligned}
&D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m})\right)\\
&= \sum_{z_{s, d} \in \{x_d, \mathbf{m}\}} q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\log\frac{q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)}{p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m})}\\
&= q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\log\frac{q(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)}{p_\theta(z_{s,d}=x_d\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m})}\\ &\qquad+ q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)\log\frac{q(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m}, x_d)}{p_\theta(z_{s,d}=\mathbf{m}\mid z_{t,d}=\mathbf{m})}\\
&= \rho_{s\mid t}\log\frac{\rho_{s\mid t}}{\rho_{s \mid t}\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,t)} + (1-\rho_{s\mid t})\log\frac{1-\rho_{s\mid t}}{1-\rho_{s \mid t}}\\
&= -\rho_{s\mid t}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,t)
\end{aligned}$$

Combining the two cases, we can write the KL term for a single coordinate as

$$
D_{KL}\left(q(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d}, x_d)\,\|\,p_\theta(z_{s,d}\mid z_{t,d})\right) = \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t,d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[-\rho_{s\mid t}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,t)\right]
$$

Next, combining the KL terms for all coordinates, we have

$$
D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid\mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t,d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[-\rho_{s\mid t}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,t)\right]
$$

### Continuous Time Limit

In this section, we discuss how we can go from a discrete time implementation of $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}$$ seen in the preliminary sections to a continuous time implementation. The discrete diffusion loss is given below, and it can be estimated using the given unbiased estimator

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}} 
&= \sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x})}\left[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\right)\right]\\
&= T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U({1,\ldots,T})}} \left[D_{KL}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)},\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_{s(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t(i)})\right)\right]\\
\end{aligned}$$

It is shown in the VDM paper [cite] that it is better to have a finer discretisation of the diffusion process because makes the variational lower bound closer to the true log-likelihood. This is good because, we ideally want to optimise the true log-likelihood, but we can only optimise the variational lower bound. Therefore, if we get closer to the true log-likelihood, we are optimising an objective closer to what we want.

Thus we see what happens when we take the limit as $$T\to\infty$$. This would mean making the grid finer, and while the number of terms in the sum increases, the gap between $$s$$ and $$t$$ becomes very small. This is done for both VDM and MDM using the KL terms derived above.

#### VDM

The main strategy to see it is to carefully use our defintions of $$s(i)$$ and $$t(i)$$ to rewrite the difference in SNR terms as a finite difference, which becomes a derivative in the limit. We have

$$\begin{aligned}
\lim_{T \to \infty} \mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}
&= \lim_{T \to \infty} T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U({1,\ldots,T})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{SNR}(s(i))-\mathrm{SNR}(t(i))\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)\right\|^2\\
&= \lim_{T \to \infty} T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U({1,\ldots,T})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{SNR}(t(i-1))-\mathrm{SNR}(t(i))\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)\right\|^2\\
&= \lim_{T \to \infty} \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U({1,\ldots,T})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{\mathrm{SNR}(t(i))-\mathrm{SNR}(t(i-1))}{1/T}\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)\right\|^2\\
&= \lim_{T \to \infty} \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U({1,\ldots,T})}} \frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{\mathrm{SNR}(t(i))-\mathrm{SNR}(t(i-1))}{t(i)-t(i-1)}\right)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)\right\|^2\\
&= \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})} \frac{1}{2}\left(-\mathrm{SNR}'(t)\left\|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t;t)\right\|^2\right)
\end{aligned}$$

#### MDM

In the case of MDM, we do exactly the same, but use a different KL term. We have

$$\begin{aligned}
\lim_{T \to \infty} \mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}
&= \lim_{T \to \infty} T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t(i),d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[-\frac{\alpha_{s(i)}-\alpha_{t(i)}}{1-\alpha_{t(i)}}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_{t(i)},t(i))\right]\\
&= \lim_{T \to \infty} T \;\mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t(i),d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[-\frac{\alpha_{t(i-1)}-\alpha_{t(i)}}{1-\alpha_{t(i)}}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_{t(i)},t(i))\right]\\
&= \lim_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t(i),d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{t(i)}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t(i)}-\alpha_{t(i-1)}}{1/T}\right)\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_{t(i)},t(i))\right]\\
&= \lim_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_{\substack{q(\mathbf{z}_{t(i)}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ i\sim U(\{1,\ldots,T\})}} \sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t(i),d}=\mathbf m\}}\left[\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{t(i)}}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t(i)}-\alpha_{t(i-1)}}{t(i)-t(i-1)}\right)\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_{t(i)},t(i))\right]\\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\substack{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1) \\ \mathbf{z}_t\sim q(\mathbf{z}_t\mid\mathbf{x})}} \left[\frac{\alpha_t'}{1-\alpha_t}\sum_{d=1}^D \mathbf{1}_{\{z_{t,d}=\mathbf m\}}\log\hat x_{\theta,d}(x_d;\mathbf z_t,t)\right]
\end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{MDM: } & \quad D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{x}) \;\Vert\; p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_s\mid \mathbf{z}_t)\right) = \frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} -\log \langle \mathbf{x}_i, \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i \rangle
\end{aligned}
$$

In the continuous time limit, $$T \to \infty$$, the gap between $$s$$ and $$t$$ becomes very small, and the finite differences become derivatives,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{VDM: } & \quad T[\operatorname{SNR}(s) - \operatorname{SNR}(t)] \to -\operatorname{SNR}'(t) \\
\text{MDM: } & \quad T\frac{\alpha_s - \alpha_t}{1 - \alpha_t} \to \frac{-\alpha_t'}{1 - \alpha_t},
\end{aligned}
$$

which can be written in terms of $$\gamma_t$$ to give the loss weights in the table.
This makes the two implementations of `loss_weight` almost identical, as both differentiate $$\gamma_t$$ with respect to $$t$$, without needing to know which noise schedule produced them.
